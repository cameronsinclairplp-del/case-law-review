# The King v Bechaud [2026] HCA 36

## One line
The High Court held that the jury separation oath under <i>Juries Act 2000</i> (Vic) s 50 can be taken early in a trial and endures throughout — it need not be re-administered or taken shortly before separation during deliberations.

## What happened
BECHAUD was arraigned on 24/07/2024 on two charges of rape and pleaded not guilty. The jury was empanelled that afternoon and, after preliminary directions and before any evidence, each juror took the separation oath under s 50 of the Juries Act 2000 (Vic). Evidence ran until 01/08/2024; the jury retired at 2.34 pm on 02/08/2024. The trial judge (Gaynor J) twice allowed the jury to separate during deliberations — on 02/08/2024 and 05/08/2024 — without reminding jurors of the separation oath. By the time deliberations began, nine days had passed since the oath was taken. On 06/08/2024 the jury acquitted on the first charge and convicted on the second. The Victorian Court of Appeal (Priest and Kaye JJA, Taylor JA dissenting) quashed the conviction, holding the oath had to be taken at a time temporally proximate and connected to the first separation during deliberations. The Crown appealed to the High Court.

## What the Court held
Section 50 does not require the separation oath to be taken at any particular time or stage of the trial. The only requirement in s 50(2) is that each separating juror has taken the oath before the judge allows separation — it operates as a precondition, not a timing rule. The Court rejected reading in words like "just" or a "temporal or factual connection" requirement. An oath, once taken, endures according to its terms and is not diluted or made futile merely by the passage of time. Jurors should be assumed to understand the oath continues to bind them (following R v Patton). The oath need not be re-administered each time the jury separates. The Court endorsed Taylor JA's dissent: the oath is taken once only, and reminding jurors they remain bound is good practice but not legally mandatory in every case. However, circumstances may arise where failure to remind jurors of the oath before separation would itself be an error or irregularity (e.g. a juror question suggesting a lack of understanding). The trial judge's discretion to allow separation is to be assessed on the circumstances immediately before each separation, but that does not mean the oath must be freshly taken. No miscarriage occurred.

## What it means for your casework
This is a Victorian statute case, so it does not directly govern your WA charging or evidence-gathering — in WA jury separation is dealt with under the Criminal Procedure Act 2004 (WA) s 111, which (unlike Victoria and Tasmania) does not require a separation oath. So the specific holding has limited direct operational effect on your casework. The value to you is contextual: it confirms the High Court's modern approach that juries routinely separate during deliberations and that sequestration is now exceptional. For your purposes, the practical point is that jury-integrity challenges based on technical oath-timing are unlikely to succeed where there is no actual suggestion a juror was influenced or failed to comply. If you are ever involved in a matter touching jury conduct, focus on whether there is any real evidence of outside influence or communication — that is what courts care about, not procedural timing technicalities.

## Verdict
AWARENESS only — this interprets Victorian legislation (s 50 Juries Act 2000 (Vic)) with no WA equivalent oath requirement, so it does not change how you investigate, gather evidence or charge.

Introduction
1

This Crown appeal concerns the correct interpretation of s 50 of the Juries
Act 2000 (Vic) ("the Act"), which empowers a judge in a jury trial to allow the jury
to separate after retiring to consider its verdict, provided that each juror first takes
the oath or makes the affirmation provided for by that section ("the separation
oath"). Section 50 provides:
"Court may allow jury to separate after retiring to consider
verdict
(1)

Subject to subsection (2) but despite any rule of law or
practice to the contrary, the court may –
(a)

allow the jury to separate; or

(b)

allow an individual juror to separate from the jury if,
in the opinion of the court, there is good reason to do
so –

after the jury has retired to consider its verdict and before the
verdict is given or the jurors are discharged.
(2)

A court may allow a jury or juror to separate in accordance
with subsection (1) only if each separating juror has taken an
oath or made an affirmation in the form of Schedule 5."

2

The prescribed form of the separation oath in Sch 5 to the Act is, relevantly,
"that you will not discuss with any person other than another member of this jury
any matter relating directly or indirectly to the evidence in this trial or the
deliberations."

3

The Crown's single ground of appeal is that the Court of Appeal of the
Supreme Court of Victoria (Priest and Kaye JJA, Taylor JA dissenting) erred in
concluding that there was a "fatal and fundamental irregularity" resulting in a
substantial miscarriage of justice in the respondent's trial because the trial judge
did not administer and therefore the jurors did not take the separation oath at a time
"both temporally proximate, and necessarily connected, with the first occasion

upon which [the] jury [was] permitted to separate while in the course of
deliberations of verdict".1
4

For the following reasons, there was no relevant error or irregularity in the
trial and, consequently, there was no substantial miscarriage of justice.2 In short,
the majority in the Court of Appeal misconstrued s 50. The appeal must be
allowed, orders 2 to 6 of the orders made by the Court of Appeal on
4 December 2025 set aside and, in their place, it must be ordered that the
respondent's appeal against conviction be dismissed.

Court of Appeal's decision

11

The respondent appealed against his conviction, including on the ground
that the trial judge erred in allowing the jury to separate after retiring to consider
its verdict without each juror having taken the separation oath shortly before
separation. The majority of the Court of Appeal allowed the appeal on this ground,
set aside the respondent's conviction and ordered a new trial.

12

Noting that the jury's taking of the separation oath is a precondition to
allowing the jury to separate after the jury has retired to consider its verdict, the
majority concluded that the separation oath is not directed to the conduct of the
jury during or after the conclusion of a trial but is specific to the conduct of the
jury's deliberations.10 The majority reasoned that it was of "particular importance"
that the separation oath "be administered at a time which necessarily connects, in
the minds and understanding of the jury, the obligation contained in [the separation
oath] with the required conduct of the jury during separation in the course of the
jury's deliberations".11

Interpretation of s 50
Text and statutory context of s 50

15

the jury to separate "after the jury has retired to consider its verdict and before the
verdict is given or the jurors are discharged". By the words "[a] court may allow a
jury or juror to separate in accordance with subsection (1) only if", s 50(2) of
the Act makes taking the separation oath by each juror a precondition to the
exercise of the trial judge's discretion to allow separation of the jury after the jury
has retired and before or during the jury's deliberations. Thus, the trial judge's

The purpose of s 50

19

The purpose of s 50 also does not support the conclusion of the majority of
the Court of Appeal that the discretion in s 50(1)(a) to allow the jury to separate is
conditional on the jurors having each taken the separation oath shortly before the
power to allow separation is first exercised.

20

It is an essential feature of the jury system that the jury should deliberate
upon its verdict uninfluenced by an outsider to the trial process. 19 Historically,
jurors were segregated together throughout the trial and until they had delivered
their verdict.20 The purpose of sequestering juries was to ensure the integrity of the
jury's verdict.21 The common law in Australia evolved away from absolute jury
sequestration and towards permitting jury members to separate from one another
while the trial was proceeding and before the jury retired to start their deliberations.
As Gaudron, Gummow and Hayne JJ observed in Brownlee v The Queen, "strict
confinement may have retarded rather than encouraged measured group
deliberation and, in former times, appeared to be calculated to pressure jurors to
reach a unanimous verdict and to do so with expedition. In modern times, the
tendency of the law, marked at the time of federation, has been to place more
reliance upon the capacity of jurors to heed the directions of the presiding judge as
to the conduct of their deliberations, to attend to the evidence and to resist outside
influences."22 Thus, it came to be accepted that this essential feature of the jury

accordance with the provision.

exposed by the lack of a precise articulation of the asserted temporal or factual
connection said to be contained in the words "has taken" in s 50(2) and the

the separation oath in temporal proximity to the jury's deliberations and (on the
other hand) the capacity of the trial judge to remind jurors of the separation oath
and other oaths and directions as and when appropriate in the circumstances of the
trial. The second alternative reading is not justified by the identified purpose: why
should it be supposed that the integrity of the jury's verdict can be protected only
by jurors taking the separation oath "just" prior to separation? Further, this
alternative wrongly implies that the separation oath must be taken by the jurors
prior to each occasion when the trial judge allows the jury to separate after retiring,
whether before or during deliberations, contrary to the assumption correctly stated
in R v Patton in relation to s 51A of the Juries Act 1967 (Vic) (the predecessor to
s 50) that:33

"To suggest that the section requires a further undertaking on oath on each

As noted, while the course considered to be "best practice" in Victoria of
the trial judge requiring jurors to take the separation oath immediately before
allowing them to separate after retiring accords with the requirements of s 50, a
position reflected in the Criminal Proceedings Manual of the Judicial College of
Victoria ("the Victorian Criminal Proceedings Manual"),34 another view is that the
purpose of protecting the integrity of a jury's verdict may well be served better by
jurors taking the separation oath before the evidence commences, as occurred in
this case. Not only would this reflect the terms of the separation oath in respect of
"the evidence in this trial", but it may also, for example, reinforce compliance with
s 78A(1)(b) of the Act, which prohibits "a juror in a trial" making an enquiry (as
defined in s 78A(5)) "for the purpose of obtaining information about a party to the
trial or any matter relevant to the trial, except in the proper exercise of his or her
functions as a juror". By s 78A(5) of the Act, "making an enquiry" includes
consulting with another person. As already noted, early administration of the
separation oath may also reinforce the preliminary directions routinely given by
trial judges about the need to avoid discussing the evidence with anyone outside

Other contextual matters
30

Finally, neither the legislative history of laws permitting jury separation nor
current practice suggests that the precondition in s 50(2) of the Act requiring jurors
to have taken the separation oath before the trial judge allows the jury to separate
should be read otherwise than in accordance with its terms. When the ability to
allow jury separation after the jury retires and before or during deliberations was
introduced in Victoria, by the Juries (Amendment) Act 1993 (Vic), the Second
Reading Speech to the Bill recorded that the experience in New South Wales of a
similar change (albeit without any requirement for a separation oath) had led to no
instances of attempted interference with a jury, that being a fear that had
underpinned the traditional practice of jury segregation.38 The Second Reading
Speech also noted the government's expectation that courts would be prepared to
allow juries to separate unless there were a real reason not to do so.39 Section 51A
was anticipated to yield cost savings and reduce the level of disruption to jurors
involved in lengthy deliberations.40

Conclusion

35

There was no error or irregularity in the trial as a result of the jurors taking
the separation oath on the trial's first day, and the trial judge subsequently
permitting the jury to separate after the jury retired and during the jury's
deliberations. The majority in the Court of Appeal erred in their interpretation of
s 50 of the Act and their consequent conclusion that there was an error or
irregularity that was a substantial miscarriage of justice.46
