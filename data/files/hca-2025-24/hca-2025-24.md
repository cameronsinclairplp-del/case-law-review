# MDP v The King [2025] HCA 24

## One line
High Court set aside MDP's child sexual offence convictions and ordered a new trial because the trial judge wrongly directed the jury it could treat innocuous "bottom slapping" evidence as proof of sexual interest, contrary to the rule in <i>Pfennig</i>.

## What happened
MDP was convicted in the District Court of Queensland of 16 counts of sexual offences against his stepdaughter, allegedly committed between 2014 and 2019 when she was aged 7 to 12. The complainant gave direct evidence of the offending. The Crown also led evidence from the complainant's sister, "K", that MDP smacked the complainant on the backside "randomly" when they "weren't doing anything wrong". That evidence was admitted without objection and was relied on by the Crown as showing MDP had a sexual interest in the complainant and was prepared to act on it. The trial judge gave the jury a propensity direction permitting that use, and defence counsel did not object (indeed sought tactical advantage from it). The Court of Appeal dismissed MDP's appeal. He appealed to the High Court.

## What the Court held
The Court was unanimous on principle. Admitting K's evidence without objection was not a "decision on a question of law" under the second limb of the common form appeal provision, so ground 4 failed. However, the trial judge did make a wrong decision on a question of law by directing the jury it could use K's evidence as propensity evidence, because that evidence could never pass the Pfennig test — there were obvious innocent (non-sexual) explanations for the smacking, so a rational view consistent with innocence remained. The Crown properly conceded this. The conduct of counsel — including failure to object or seeking forensic advantage — is irrelevant to whether a wrong decision was made; the trial judge alone is responsible for a correct summing up. The Court confirmed a materiality threshold applies to the second limb: unless the error is fundamental, the appellant must show it could realistically have affected the jury's reasoning to guilt. The majority (Gageler CJ, Gordon, Steward and Edelman JJ) held the direction was material and the proviso could not be applied because contested credit issues and an incomplete record meant guilt was not shown to be inevitable. The minority (Gleeson, Jagot and Beech-Jones JJ) would have dismissed, finding the direction's protective elements meant it was not material.

## What it means for your casework
When you build a sexual offence case, be careful about relying on equivocal conduct as "evidence of sexual interest". Evidence like non-sexual physical contact (smacking a child's bottom) that has an obvious innocent explanation will not pass the Pfennig test and cannot properly be used for propensity reasoning — leading it as propensity evidence risks quashing an otherwise strong conviction. Identify and record whether uncharged-act or "relationship" evidence has a genuinely probative, exclusively-sexual character before it is characterised as showing sexual interest. Do not assume weak "context" material strengthens a case; here it produced a retrial. Note also that a defence failure to object does not cure a wrongful use of such evidence — the risk sits with the prosecution and the trial, not just defence tactics.

## Verdict
AWARENESS — this is mainly appellate law on the common form appeal provision, but it carries a practical warning about how "sexual interest" / propensity evidence must be assessed against Pfennig before it is relied on.

GORDON AND STEWARD JJ. This appeal turns on the second limb of
the "common form" appeal provision for appeals from a conviction on indictment
in s 668E(1) of the Criminal Code (Qld),17 which requires the Court of Appeal of
the Supreme Court of Queensland to allow an appeal against conviction if it is of
the opinion "that the judgment of the court of trial should be set aside on the ground
of the wrong decision of any question of law". If there is a wrong decision on
a question of law, then the conviction is set aside if that wrong decision was
fundamental18 to the trial, or, subject to the application of the proviso,19 if that
wrong decision was material, in the sense that it could realistically have affected
the reasoning of the tribunal of fact to verdict in the trial that was had.
The applicable principles in relation to the second limb are set out in the reasons
of Gleeson, Jagot and Beech-Jones JJ.20

Section 668E(1) of the Criminal Code (Qld) is in a common form. It
relevantly provides that on an appeal against conviction, the court "shall allow the
appeal if it is of opinion that the verdict of the jury should be set aside on the

"[(i)] that [the verdict] is unreasonable, or can not be supported having

whether the misdirection is characterised as an 'error of law', as a 'miscarriage of
justice', or as a 'wrong decision of any question of law'".54

A decision on a question of law
56

As Gleeson, Jagot and Beech-Jones JJ explain, "the natural and ordinary
meaning of a decision on a question of law involves some determination or
response to a question of law by a trial judge that has legal effect in the trial".

57

In order to determine whether there is a decision on a question of law, the
focus is therefore upon actions taken by the trial judge with legal effect (namely,
determinations and responses). There is no utility in an attempt to divine any
thought process of the trial judge. Nor is there any utility in an attempt to identify
whether any question of law had been raised by the parties expressly or by
implication. Although the absence of any determination or response by a trial judge
to the admission of inadmissible evidence will not constitute a "decision" (hence
the admission of the bottom slapping evidence in this case involved no decision),
an error made by the trial judge in their directions to the jury can constitute a wrong
decision on a question of law even without any request for a particular direction or
for a re-direction.63 A mere failure by a trial judge to give a particular direction, by
itself, will not ordinarily involve any determination or response to a question of
law by the trial judge that has a legal effect in the trial. But such a failure might
mean that a direction that was actually given by the trial judge, or facts that were
summarised by the trial judge, involved an error of law in that the jury were
permitted the liberty to engage in impermissible reasoning.

of law might be expressed as whether a propensity direction should have been
given. Alternatively, the question of law might be expressed as whether the trial

The second step in engaging the second limb of the common form appeal
provision arises if there is a decision on a question of law. If there is a decision on
a question of law, the appellant must then establish that the decision was "wrong".

104

The third step in engaging the second limb of the common form appeal
provision arises if it is shown there was a wrong decision on a question of law, in
which case the appellant must show that the wrong decision involved an error that
was either fundamental to the trial or, if not fundamental, material in the relevant
sense.

judgment of the court of trial should be set aside on the ground of the wrong
decision of any question of law" (emphasis added). The common form appeal

The second limb and the conduct of counsel

109

If the evidence the subject of a particular decision on a question of law was

refused or given over opposition, then no question of the conduct of counsel who

arise. Further, while the decisions of counsel will often provide the context in
