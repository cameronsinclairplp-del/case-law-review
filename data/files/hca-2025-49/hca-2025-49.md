# The King v Tsalkos [2025] HCA 49

_Held for review — the fact-check found problems with this write-up (pipeline/audit.py, run by the daily bot and by add_case.py --audit); treat every statement below as unverified until the hold is cleared._

## One line
Distress evidence given by a complainant's mother at the time the complaint was made is admissible circumstantial evidence that the sexual offending occurred, and juries need no special "unreliable" warning about it.

## What happened
TSALKOS was convicted in the County Court of Victoria of two kidnappings, two aggravated rapes and four counts of procuring acts of gross indecency against two teenage complainants, AB and JJ, over events in 1987. AB's mother gave evidence, without objection, that she found AB "very very distressed" at the hospital at the time AB told her she had been raped. The trial judge directed the jury it could use the distress as indirect/circumstantial evidence supporting AB's account, but only if satisfied the distress was caused by the offending and not another reason (such as AB concealing sex work or lying to police). The Victorian Court of Appeal majority allowed TSALKOS's appeal, holding the distress was "intractably neutral" and could not support the account, and ordered a retrial. The King appealed to the High Court.

## What the Court held
The appeal was allowed and the convictions restored. Applying R v Churchill, the High Court held that evidence a complainant was distressed at the time of making a pre-trial complaint is ordinarily relevant under s 55 of the Evidence Act 2008 (Vic) and admissible under s 56 to prove the offending occurred, on the basis the jury may find a causal connection between the distress and the alleged offending. The Court of Appeal erred by separating the distress from the content of the complaint, by treating "independent", "indirect" and "circumstantial" evidence as interchangeable, and by applying an abolished common law corroboration rule. The fact there could be another explanation for the distress does not make it irrelevant; that goes to weight for the jury. The distress had substantial probative value and s 137 was not engaged. The prosecutor's description of the evidence as "independent" was not a miscarriage — it meant only that it came from a source other than AB, not that it was independent corroboration.

## What it means for your casework
This is a Victorian decision on Victorian statutes, so it is awareness only for WA work, but the reasoning is instructive. It confirms that a complainant's observable distress at the time of complaint is not "neutral" filler — where witnessed by another person it can be evidence that the offence occurred, not just a boost to credit. When you take statements from a person present when a complaint was first made (a parent, friend, first responder), record their observations of the complainant's emotional and physical state carefully and precisely, and note the timing relative to the complaint. Do not assume such evidence is worthless because innocent explanations exist — that is a jury question. Check how WA's Evidence Act 1906 and Criminal Procedure Act 2004 treat this before relying on the specific holdings.

## Verdict
AWARENESS: it sharpens how you gather and value distress/first-complaint evidence, but it turns on Victorian legislation, so confirm the WA position before applying it operationally.

Third, the majority's description of the distress evidence as "intractably
neutral" does not reflect a proper application of s 55 of the Evidence Act. Instead,
it reflects the application of the superseded historical evidentiary rule just noted.
Section 55(1) merely provides that evidence is relevant where the evidence, if
accepted, "could rationally affect (directly or indirectly) the assessment of the
probability of the existence of a fact in issue in the proceeding". The circumstance
that there could be another explanation for the distress evidence does not mean that
the test of relevance in s 55 is not satisfied. Instead, consideration must be given
to whether there is a process of reasoning by which the evidence could rationally
affect the assessment of the probabilities.53 In circumstances where the complaint

complaint,54 the test of relevance in s 55 was satisfied.

Section 137 of the Evidence Act provides that, "[i]n a criminal proceeding,
