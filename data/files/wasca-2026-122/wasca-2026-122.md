# Lagdon v The State of Western Australia [2026] WASCA 122

## One line
Court of Appeal upholds a total effective 7 years 9 months for a teacher who groomed and sexually abused a student and used online deception to procure and expose three interstate 12-13yo girls.

## What happened
Harry Sing Wan LAGDON, a high school art teacher in a regional WA town, pleaded guilty to 25 sexually motivated counts against four child victims. Counts 1-15 involved his year 11 student Tanja (a pseudonym), aged 15 then 16 during 2021, whom he groomed with texts, gifts and escalating contact before repeated indecent dealing and unprotected penile-vaginal, oral and other penetration on school grounds, in a shed, in his car and at his apartment, plus hundreds of explicit and degrading images and videos over about 18 months. Counts 16-25 involved three Queensland girls aged 12 or 13 (ERO, MO, LRMR) whom he contacted online while posing as a 14, 15 or 16-year-old boy, sending explicit messages and videos, exposing them to indecent matter, procuring images (he successfully procured LRMR to send explicit images), and possessing child exploitation material. He was sentenced by CURWOOD DCJ in the District Court to a total effective 7 years 9 months (count 9, unprotected penetration of Tanja, as head sentence). He appealed the count 9 sentence as manifestly excessive and the total effective sentence as breaching the first limb of the totality principle.

## What the Court held
The Court granted leave on grounds 2 and 3 but dismissed the appeal. The sentences were firm but not unreasonable or unjust, so no implied error was shown. The Court held that consent is not an element of the s 322 (or s 321) offences, and it is not meaningful to describe a child under a teacher's care, supervision or authority as a 'willing participant' or the situation as a 'consensual relationship'; any willingness rises no higher than the absence of an aggravating factor. It stressed the inherent power and trust imbalance in the teacher-student relationship and that general deterrence is important. It noted counts 4, 6 and 8 involved a refusal being overridden and an element of force. It confirmed that sentences for child sexual offending have firmed up, so caution is needed with pre-2010 comparables, and current standards are best drawn from recent decisions. It found the present offending more serious than Visser or EKO given the four victims, unprotected penetration, scale and derogatory content of the communications, sexual interest in underage girls and age deception.

## What it means for your casework
This is a sentencing appeal, so it does not change how you investigate, but it is useful context. When you deal with a teacher-student or authority-figure case, do not let 'it was consensual' or 'a relationship' derail the file — consent is not an element of these offences and the child's apparent willingness is legally close to irrelevant. Gather and preserve the grooming trail: texts, gifts, images and videos, downloads from devices, and evidence of deception about age in online offending — these were central aggravating features. Document every count and occasion separately rather than treating repeat conduct as one event, as the persistence and number of victims drove the outcome. Where an offender poses as a peer online, secure the platform and device evidence establishing the false identity. Note that any element of force or an initial refusal (as in counts 4, 6, 8) is worth capturing clearly in statements as it adds to seriousness.

## Verdict
AWARENESS only — this is a sentence appeal confirming principles; it does not alter investigative or charging practice, though it reinforces how to frame consent and grooming evidence.

## Flags (verify before relying)
- Charges span multiple Criminal Code (WA) provisions (s 204B(2)(a), s 204B(3)(a), s 220, s 320(3), s 321(4), s 322(2), s 322(4)) — check the specific section against the count before relying on it.
- Queensland conduct was prosecuted under the Criminal Code (WA); the judgment does not explain the jurisdictional basis, so do not assume it for other files.
- Sentencing discounts referenced s 9AA of the Sentencing Act 1995 (WA) (25% plea reduction) — verify current provisions if relevant.

Legal principles
57

1

Both grounds of appeal alleged an implied error in the exercise of
the sentencing discretion. The legal principles governing appeals
contending that error should be inferred on the basis that an individual
sentence is manifestly excessive or inadequate, or that a total effective
sentence infringes the totality principle, are well established and were
outlined by this court in Kabambi v The State of Western Australia, as
follows:1
(1)

Sentencing is a discretionary exercise. An appellate court can
intervene only if the appellant demonstrates either an express or
implied material error. Express error involves acting on a wrong
principle, for example by mistaking the law or facts or taking
into account an irrelevant matter. Implied error arises where the
end result is so unreasonable or unjust that the court must
conclude that a substantial wrong has occurred. Thus, an
appellate court cannot substitute its own opinion for that of the
sentencing court merely because the appellate court would have
exercised a sentencing discretion differently.

(2)

In order to determine whether a sentence for an individual
offence is manifestly excessive or inadequate, the offence
should be viewed in light of the maximum sentence prescribed
by law for the crime, the standards of sentencing customarily
imposed with respect to it, the place that the criminal conduct
occupies in the scale of seriousness of crimes of that type, and
the offender's personal circumstances.

(3)

The first limb of the totality principle requires that the total
effective sentence imposed on an offender who has committed
multiple offences bears a proper relationship to the overall
criminality involved in all of the offences (including those, if
any, in respect of which the offender is still serving or is yet to

Kabambi v The State of Western Australia [2019] WASCA 44 [21].

[2026] WASCA 122
JUDGMENT OF THE COURT

serve a term of imprisonment), viewed in their entirety, having
regard to all relevant facts and circumstances including those
referable to the offender personally (and including, for example,
the desirability of accommodating any wish to rehabilitate), all
relevant sentencing factors and the total effective sentences
imposed in comparable cases.

58

(4)

The range of sentences customarily imposed for a crime does
not establish the range of a sound exercise of the sentencing
discretion. Sentences customarily imposed in comparable cases
provide a yardstick or reference point for ensuring broad
consistency in sentencing, bearing in mind the scope for
significant variations in relevant sentencing factors, and that
there is no single correct sentence. What is important is the
unifying principles which sentences imposed in comparable
cases reveal and reflect.

(5)

When this court dismisses an appeal against sentence and when
it resentences on a successful appeal, its decision does not fix
the upper or lower limit of the range.

(6)

Where there is a challenge on totality grounds, the severity of a
sentence imposed on an individual count generally falls to be
assessed in light of the sentences imposed in respect of the other
counts and its contribution to the total effective sentence. A
heavy individual sentence (which is not manifestly excessive)
may be softened by an order that it be served concurrently with
sentences imposed in relation to the other counts. A relatively
light sentence (which is not manifestly inadequate) may, as a
practical matter, have increased severity if it is ordered to be
served cumulatively. The real question is whether the total
effective sentence is unreasonable or plainly unjust.
