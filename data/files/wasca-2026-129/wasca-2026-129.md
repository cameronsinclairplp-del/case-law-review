# MRV v SNW [No 2] [2026] WASCA 129

## One line
Court of Appeal dismisses a self-represented parent's challenge to family law property and change-of-surname orders, confirming the House v The King approach to reviewing discretionary decisions.

## What happened
Two women were in a same-sex de facto relationship for over 10 years (2011 to separation on 19/11/2021) and had two children. After a 5-day trial, COHEN J of the Family Court of WA made property adjustment orders (the appellant to pay the respondent $187,967 out of the parties' interests, chiefly the equity in a Joondalup property) and a parenting order hyphenating the children's surname. MRV, self-represented on appeal, ran 12 grounds (one abandoned) challenging: the characterisation of $118,636 paid by her mother into the parties' joint account towards the house; the refusal to use a 'two-pool' approach; the inclusion/exclusion of certain liabilities; a 12.5% adjustment for the respondent under s 205ZD factors; and the surname order. The bias ground (ground 7) was abandoned.

## What the Court held
The appeal was dismissed. The court held the primary judge made no material error in a House v The King sense. On the $118,636, the presumption of advancement did not arise (no resulting trust claim by the mother, and the payment was to both parties jointly with a 'gift-family' notation); where evidence of actual intention exists, the presumption/counter-presumption is of practical significance only in rare cases, and intention is an ordinary question of fact on the balance of probabilities (Bosanac). Reliance on the mother's contemporaneous letter, despite no oral evidence from her, was not impermissible use of hearsay. A single-pool approach was reasonably open; the appellant had to show a two-pool approach was clearly appropriate, and did not. Excluding the NZ student loan (uncertain, unlikely to be enforced) and offsetting post-separation liabilities was open (Biltoft, Prince). The 12.5% adjustment was well open given the significant income and earning-capacity disparity. The surname hyphenation was child-focused and in the children's best interests under s 66C, not a mere symbolic gesture.

## What it means for your casework
This is a family law property and parenting appeal with little direct bearing on your criminal casework. Its value to you is background awareness of appellate evidence and proof principles you will meet across the courts: findings of fact are decided on the balance of probabilities in civil matters; Briginshaw calibrates the strength of evidence needed for serious allegations but does not raise the standard; a Jones v Dunkel adverse inference can flow from an unexplained failure to call an available witness; and contemporaneous documents are routinely used to infer intention as objective fact even where the author is not called. Note also that the primary judge found MRV intended to misrepresent her financial circumstances to Services Australia — a passing credibility finding, not a charge, but a reminder that adverse findings in civil proceedings can surface as intelligence.

The presumption of advancement is commonly applied in relation
to the doctrine of presumptive resulting trusts, sometimes being
described as an 'exception' to the presumption of a resulting trust:

1.

A trust of a legal estate in property taken in the name of another
is taken to 'result' to the person who advances the purchase
money - this is the 'presumption of a resulting trust' [12]. The
presumption can be rebutted by evidence and cannot prevail
over the actual intention of the party paying the purchase price
as established by the overall evidence [13].

2.

The 'presumption of advancement' allows an inference as to
intention to be drawn from the fact of certain relationships.
These relationships include the relationship of parent-child.
The so-called presumption, which on one view is not a
presumption at all, may be better understood as providing the
absence of any reason for assuming that a trust arose. It too
may be rebutted by evidence of actual intention; and, indeed, is
especially weak in a contemporary setting [14] - [15], [22].

Gageler J referred to the presumption of advancement as a
'counter-presumption':
The counter-presumption arises where a contributor and a purchaser
were in a recognised category of relationship. The archetypal category
is where the contributor was a husband and the purchaser was his wife.
The counter-presumption is that the contributor and the purchaser
intended the contribution to the purchase price to have been made and
received as a gift, for the purchaser's 'advancement' [52].

Gageler J went on to summarise the principles that emerge from
the authorities. Relevantly, the presumption of a resulting trust is a
presumption of fact that yields to an actual intention to the contrary, the
latter being found on the balance of probabilities as an inference drawn
from the totality of the evidence [64]. The counter-presumption of
advancement is not really a presumption at all - it being a 'circumstance
of evidence' (Gordon and Edelman JJ effectively agreed with this
characterisation [115]). The counter-presumption is enough to negative
the presumption that arises from the bare fact of contribution to the
purchase price. But the circumstance of the relationship 'will not be
considered alone if other evidence going to intention is adduced and
will then simply be weighed in the overall evidentiary mix' [65].

In all cases, '[w]hether any, and if so what, inference is … to be
drawn about the actual intention of the contributor and the purchaser

probabilities' [66]. Importantly, as we will come to in more detail, the
presumption and counter-presumption have a lesser role where
evidence relevant to intention is adduced. They are of practical
significance only in 'rare cases' [67].

Grounds 3 and 4: did the primary judge's discretion miscarry in not
applying a 'two-pool approach'?
75

By ground 3, and part of ground 4, the appellant alleges that the
primary judge erred by failing to consider or apply a two-pool
approach. The initial part of the appellant's complaint is misconceived.
The primary judge expressly considered whether to adopt a two-pool
approach [167] - [174]. The primary judge decided not to adopt a
two-pool approach for reasons reproduced at [30] above. Thus
resolution of ground 3 and this aspect of ground 4 requires
consideration of whether the primary judge's exercise of discretion
miscarried in refusing to adopt a two-pool approach.

76

A 'two-pool' approach involves identifying and isolating particular
property into a separate pool. Such a separate pool is often comprised
of items of property that one party made little or no contribution
towards. Hence there are two notional pools for the exercise mandated
by s 205ZG of the Act. It is not uncommon for the court to assess
contributions on the basis of a two-pool approach. There is no doubt
that such an approach is open to a trial judge in the exercise of his or
her discretion. It might, for example, be appropriate to apply a

[2026] WASCA 129
JUDGMENT OF THE COURT

two-pool approach where an inheritance has been received by one party
late in the parties' relationship or post-separation. Also, as the Full
Court of the Family Court of Australia stated in In the Marriage of
Lenehan, there are 'cases where one approach or the other is clearly
appropriate and a failure by the trial judge to adopt that approach may
demonstrate error'.11
77

At trial, counsel for the appellant argued for a two-pool
approach - one which isolated the Joondalup property from the other
property available for division between the parties. This would have
required the primary judge to assess the parties' contributions separately
with reference to the two pools. Senior counsel for the respondent
contended that the parties' contributions should be assessed globally by
reference to the whole of the property available for division.

and the respondent; the $118,636 was received shortly before
separation; the $118,636 was traceable into a single transaction; and the
$118,636 was used towards acquiring the Joondalup property (being the
principal asset of the relationship). So stated it should be apparent that
the final two matters are covered by the first matter.

considerations at both the outset of the change of name reasons [291]
and ultimately in being satisfied that it was in the children's best
interests for their surnames to be hyphenated [313].

143

The other material considerations that the primary judge is alleged
to have failed to take into account may be dealt with quite shortly.

144

The primary judge was cognisant of and paid due regard to the
appellant's ongoing role in the children's lives and identity formation.
That consideration arose within the rubric of the principles mentioned
by her Honour [292](c) - (e), [302]. In this respect the primary judge
acknowledged the children's extremely positive relationships with both
of their parents [303] (see also [309]) and the respondent's
commendable view that, irrespective of biology, the appellant and the
respondent will always be the children's parents [304]. So too
her Honour took into account the long-term significance of name
continuity and its role in maintaining familial bonds. See [313](b), (d).

145

In addition to the foregoing, the primary judge expressly
acknowledged that Sam had been known by and had identified with the
appellant's surname for his entire life and that this was a 'significant
consideration' [300]. The primary judge also referred to the appellant's
concern that her connection with the children might be undermined if
their surname changed [312] (a concern that was lessened in any event
given the respondent's acceptance of the appellant's continuing role as a
parent [313](a) - see also [313](d)).

146

Finally, there is the appellant's suggestion that the primary judge
failed to take into account the cultural and emotional implications of
severing the children's connection to the appellant. There is no basis
for this complaint. The primary judge acknowledged the appellant's
concern that her surname might be omitted over time were it to be
placed last. To alleviate this concern the primary judge was satisfied
that it was appropriate and in the best interests of the children to make
an order changing the children's surname in the form 'appellant's
surname - respondent's surname' [316] - [317].

147

Ground 12 is entirely without merit and must be dismissed.

148

As pleaded in ground 11, the relevant factors that the primary
judge is alleged to have not properly considered are:
the children's best interests, cultural identity, and applicable legal
principles.

149

So stated ground 11, even when treated as raising whether the
change of surname decision is unreasonable or plainly unjust upon the
facts, has immediate difficulties. It is apparent from what has been said
in dealing with ground 12 that each of the matters relied on by the
appellant in support of ground 11 was properly taken into account by
the primary judge in the exercise of the discretion. The appellant
nevertheless contends that it was not reasonably open to the primary
judge to conclude that the change of surname was in the best interests
of the children.

150

In written submissions the appellant says that the primary judge
failed to adequately consider four matters. First, the significance of the
children's existing surname in connection with their cultural, familial
and personal identity. Second, the potential confusion or emotional
impact on the children from a unilateral surname change. Third, the
absence of consent from the appellant. Fourth, whether the proposed
change was supported by objective evidence as being in the best
interests of the children (appellant's submissions, par 11.2. See also
par 11.5).

151

We will come back to the last consideration. It is at the heart of
ground 11. It is plain from what we have already said that, contrary to
the appellant's submission, the primary judge gave significant weight to
the first consideration. It was a matter that favoured retention of the
status quo.
Little weight was properly given to the second
consideration. The primary judge recorded that Dr Jones did not
believe that Sam would be concerned should the court make an order
hyphenating his surname [311]. In this respect Dr Jones had observed
that 'undercurrents around biology and genetic links are relevant for
parents but less so for children and young people' [312]. The primary
judge concluded on the basis of the evidence as a whole that:
I am not convinced that a change in surname will cause [Sam] or
[Sarah] undue confusion or embarrassment. The children embrace an
unconventional family system, as reflected by the fact they refer to [the
appellant] as 'Daddy' without hesitation. Parental separation is
unfortunately common, and it is unlikely their peers will be unsettled by
a surname change that simply reflects their other parent's name
[313](c).

[2026] WASCA 129
JUDGMENT OF THE COURT
152

No appellable error has been demonstrated in that finding. It was
supported by Dr Jones' evidence, which the primary judge accepted. In
doing so the primary judge was conscious of the circumstance that she
had no current understanding of Sam's views on the matter
[314] - [315]. The primary judge's reluctance to require that Sam be
consulted was understandable, and can in no way bespeak appellable
error, in circumstances where Dr Jones had given evidence as to Sam's
reluctance to discuss his experiences in the two households and
apparent apprehension about being perceived as partisan [310].

153

The suggestion that the primary judge failed to adequately
consider the appellant's opposition to the change of surname overlooks
that in her reasons the primary judge took some five pages to explain in
detail why the change of surname order should be made
notwithstanding the appellant's lack of consent to the change.

154

In oral submissions the appellant complained of the manner in
which the discretion was exercised and the reasoning process that the
primary judge employed. The appellant placed store on the primary
judge having made no finding that the retention of the appellant's
surname would cause confusion, embarrassment or psychological harm
to the children. The appellant contended that the primary judge
deployed the surname change as a preventative measure in the context
of parental conflict where the evidence was that the children were wellconnected to both parents. The appellant also relied on Sam's long-held
identity - he having carried the appellant's surname exclusively through
childhood and into early adolescence. The appellant submitted that
altering a long-established identity required a clear, child-centred
justification grounded in evidence of likely benefit and demonstrated
detriment were the existing surname to be retained (appeal ts 61 - 64).

155

Change of name decisions are fact specific: Flanagan v
Handcock [35]. In Chapman and Palmer the Full Court of the Family
Court identified the relevant factors to which the court should have
regard in the following terms (471):
(a)

The welfare of the child is the paramount consideration.

(b)

The short and long term effects of any change in the child's
surname.

(c)

Any embarrassment likely to be experienced by the child if its
name is different from that of the parent with custody or care
and control.

JUDGMENT OF THE COURT

(d)

Any confusion of identity which may arise for the child if his or
her name is changed or is not changed.

(e)

The effect which any change in surname may have on the
relationship between the child and the parent whose name the
child bore during the marriage.

(f)

The effect of frequent or random changes of name.

156

Other potentially relevant factors are catalogued in Flanagan v
Handcock [36].

157

In the present case the primary judge concluded that it would be in
the children's best interests to have a hyphenated surname which would
reflect their ongoing relationship with both parents. Four reasons were
given for that conclusion. We have already reproduced one of those
reasons at [151] above. The primary judge did not accept that the
hyphenated surname would cause the children undue confusion or
embarrassment. The other three reasons given by the primary judge
were:
[F]irst, I accept Dr Jones' evidence that the greatest risk to [Sam] and
[Sarah] is a psychological one, stemming from their parents' unrelenting
conflict. His insights about ameliorating against weaponizing, and the
importance of creating equity wherever possible, were particularly
compelling, especially given the risk of alienation within this family.
While I have no concern about [the respondent] … I am persuaded that
hyphenating the children's surnames may help counteract [the
appellant's] undermining of their relationship with [the respondent],
thereby reducing the risk of alienation.
[S]econd, in a modern Australian society, both parents have a legitimate
interest in their children's surnames reflecting their own …
…
[F]inally, I accept Dr Jones' evidence regarding the strength of [the
appellant's] relationship with [Sam], which aligns with my own
impression. I am not persuaded that hyphenating the children's
surnames would in any way diminish their bond with her, provided [the
appellant] shields them from any hurt, disappointment or anger she may
feel about this decision. I have inferred from the totality of Dr Jones'
evidence that the children will be best positioned to adapt and manage
this change if both parents support and discuss decisions constructively,
without undermining one another [313].

158

It may be accepted that, in large part, the change of surname order
providing for a hyphenated surname was a preventative measure. The

[2026] WASCA 129
JUDGMENT OF THE COURT

primary judge considered it to be in the best interests of the children to
do so to preserve the children's relationship with each of their parents.
This provided a positive reason for the change to a hyphenated
surname. Otherwise the matters referred to by the primary judge were
substantially directed to satisfying herself that the contemplated change
in surname would not adversely affect the children. The primary judge
also referred to the parties' legitimate interests. We would give that
consideration little or no weight where it was not suggested that the
parties' legitimate interests impacted on the best interests of the
children.
159

While, in that latter respect, we take a different view on the force
of one consideration to which the primary judge gave weight, we are
satisfied that it was well open to her Honour to harbour a concern that
there was an unacceptable risk that the appellant might take steps that
undermined the children's relationship with the respondent. The basis
for that concern is developed at primary reasons [306] - [308]. It would
not assist the continued need for the parties to engage in cooperative
parenting of their children to repeat those findings of the primary judge.
It suffices to state that the primary judge accepted that there had been
some undermining of the children's relationship with their mother.
Those factual findings are not challenged in the appeal.
In
circumstances where such undermining had occurred it was reasonably
open to conclude, as the primary judge did, that hyphenating the
children's surname was in their best interests insofar as it would tend to
counteract any conduct or behaviour which might impact adversely on
the relationship between the respondent and the children.

160

The position might be otherwise had the primary judge made the
hyphenation order as a symbolic response to parental equality. Such a
consideration will ordinarily be insufficient by itself. Here, however,
the change of surname order was child-focused - hyphenation was
ordered as a measure reasonably directed to protecting the children's
relationships with both parents.

161

Ground 11 fails. The change of surname decision is not
unreasonable or plainly unjust upon the facts as found by the primary
judge. To the contrary, on the facts as found it was well open to the
primary judge to conclude that it was in the children's best interests for
their surname to be hyphenated in the form 'appellant's
surname - respondent's surname'.
