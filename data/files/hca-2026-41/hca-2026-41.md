# O'Connell v Director of Public Prosecutions Director of Public Prosecutions v O'Connell [2026] HCA 41

## One line
High Court restores O'CONNELL's murder conviction, holding the ACT Court of Appeal wrongly found the verdict unreasonable by ignoring jury advantages and treating a circumstantial case piecemeal.

## What happened
Michael O'CONNELL was convicted by an ACT jury of the murder of Danielle JORDAN, who died from head injuries after falling from the bonnet of his moving car during a confrontation on 15/04/2022 (she died 17/04/2022). The prosecution case was that he deliberately drove with her on the bonnet, recklessly indifferent to the probability of causing death, with manslaughter (unlawful and dangerous act) as an alternative. A key witness was Ms X, 13, who saw JORDAN on the bonnet and heard a screech then a 'doof'. CCTV (poor quality, with sound) and collision-reconstruction evidence were also led, and the jury conducted a view of the scene. The Court of Appeal majority set aside the murder verdict as unreasonable and substituted a manslaughter verdict; the Chief Justice dissented. Both sides appealed to the High Court.

## What the Court held
The DPP's appeal was allowed and O'CONNELL's dismissed. The Court held the Court of Appeal majority failed to make full allowance for the advantages enjoyed by the jury, especially the jury's view of the scene and its ability to assess O'CONNELL's expressions, tone and reaction on the CCTV, when drawing inferences about his state of mind. It also erred by considering the circumstantial case piecemeal — assessing each circumstance in isolation, discarding those that alone did not prove reckless indifference, rather than weighing the combined effect of all the circumstances. The Court distinguished R v Baden-Clay: here the innocence hypothesis arose on the same primary facts the prosecution relied on (only the inference of reckless indifference differed), so it was not mere conjecture. It was legitimate for an appeal court to raise an unargued issue with the parties provided procedural fairness is given. The Court also held Supreme Court Act 1933 (ACT) s 37O(1)(d) empowers entry of a guilty verdict to an alternative offence. The matter was remitted to the Court of Appeal.

## What it means for your casework
This reinforces how appellate courts review circumstantial cases, which affects how you should build one. A circumstantial case is proved by the accumulation of all the evidence, not by any single strand — individual facts that look innocent in isolation can together exclude innocence. Gather and present the full picture: the accused's emotional state, prior conduct, manner of driving/acting, knowledge of the victim, and post-event conduct, so the combined effect supports the required mental element. Note the reckless-indifference murder element here turns on foresight of the probability (likelihood) of death, not mere possibility — relevant when framing allegations about an accused's state of mind. This is an ACT case on ACT legislation; the WA equivalent appeal provisions are the Criminal Appeals Act 2004 (WA). Nothing here changes your day-to-day procedure, but it is a useful statement of circumstantial-evidence and jury-advantage principles.

## Verdict
AWARENESS — a High Court restatement of circumstantial-evidence and appellate-review principles on ACT legislation; it sharpens understanding of circumstantial proof and the reckless-murder mental element but does not change WA investigative procedure.

GAGELER CJ, GORDON, STEWARD, GLEESON, JAGOT AND BEECHJONES JJ. In all Australian jurisdictions a court must allow an appeal against
conviction if the court is satisfied that the verdict of guilty should be set aside on
the ground that it is unreasonable or cannot be supported having regard to the
evidence. 1 The definitive statement of the approach appellate courts must adopt in
determining such a ground is in the judgment of Mason CJ, Deane, Dawson and
Toohey JJ in M v The Queen. 2 In determining such a ground, the appellate court
must ask itself "whether it thinks that upon the whole of the evidence it was open
to the jury to be satisfied beyond reasonable doubt that the accused was guilty". 3
In doing so the appellate court must make "full allowance for the advantages
enjoyed by the jury" 4 in witnessing the evidence unfold at the trial. 5 Further, in
considering whether a guilty verdict in a circumstantial case was unreasonable, the
appellate court, like the jury, must consider all the circumstances established by
the evidence and weigh them in determining whether an inference consistent with
innocence was reasonably open on the evidence. 6

The evidence at trial

10

Mr O'Connell and Ms Jordan were in a relationship that was described by
the Court of Appeal as "tumultuous". On the evening of 14 April 2022,
Mr O'Connell stayed at Ms Jordan's home at 6 Coutts Place, Melba. At around
2.00 am the following morning Mr O'Connell and Ms Jordan argued and,
following a physical struggle, Mr O'Connell left Ms Jordan's home.

11

Mr O'Connell was alleged to have then returned to the home at around
4.00 am and started arguing with Ms Jordan again. Mr O'Connell attempted to
leave but Ms Jordan tried to prevent him from doing so by placing herself on the
bonnet of Mr O'Connell's car. The prosecution contended that Mr O'Connell
nevertheless drove off at speed and turned left out of Coutts Place and drove down
Alfred Hill Drive. The prosecution contended that Mr O'Connell drove

approximately 200 m down Alfred Hill Drive when Ms Jordan fell from the bonnet
of the vehicle, suffering fatal head injuries. Ms Jordan was taken to hospital by
Mr O'Connell. She was declared deceased on 17 April 2022.
12

Crucial to the prosecution case was the evidence of Ms X, who was 13 years
old at the time of the incident and present at Ms Jordan's home during the
confrontation. Although there were said to be variations in Ms X's accounts of
what happened, the effect of her evidence was that, when Ms Jordan would not
remove herself from the bonnet, Ms X stood in front of Mr O'Connell's car to stop
him driving away. Ms X stated that Mr O'Connell drove slowly along Coutts Place
with Ms Jordan on the bonnet towards Ms X in the direction of the intersection
with Alfred Hill Drive while she (Ms X) walked backwards. Ms X said that when
Mr O'Connell's car reached the top of Coutts Place near the intersection with
Alfred Hill Drive, he reversed back down Coutts Place past Ms Jordan's place
before accelerating up the street towards Ms X at a speed which caused Ms X to
jump out of the way. Ms X said that Mr O'Connell then turned left at the
intersection of Coutts Place and Alfred Hill Drive with Ms Jordan still on the
bonnet of his car.

13

Ms X said that although she could not see Mr O'Connell's car after it turned
into Alfred Hill Drive, she heard it and ran after the car before stopping to walk
and catch her breath. Ms X said she then heard "a screech and, [a sound] like, a
doof", which Ms X perceived to be the noise of a body falling on the ground. Ms X
did not see Ms Jordan fall from the car. Ms X said that, as she approached
Mr O'Connell's car on Alfred Hill Drive, she saw him leave the vehicle, walk to
the back of the car, collect Ms Jordan from the roadway and put her in the
passenger seat. Ms X recalled Mr O'Connell saying, amongst other things, "I'm
sorry, mate, I didn't mean to do it" and "I'm sorry Bub, I'm so sorry, I didn't mean
to do that". Ms X said she told Mr O'Connell to take Ms Jordan to the hospital.

14

The first judgment

27

The original focus of Mr O'Connell's unreasonable verdict ground was the
alleged unreliability of the evidence of Ms X and the sufficiency of proof of the
presence of Ms Jordan on the bonnet of Mr O'Connell's car when he was driving
and she fell. However, after the Court of Appeal had initially reserved its judgment,
the Court invited submissions from the parties addressing three questions, namely:
(i) if it was accepted that it was open to the jury to find that Ms Jordan was on the
bonnet of the vehicle when she fell and suffered fatal injuries, ought the jury have
entertained a reasonable doubt as to Mr O'Connell's reckless indifference to the
probability of death; (ii) was proof of Mr O'Connell's reckless indifference in those
circumstances an issue that arose at the trial; and (iii) was the sufficiency of the
proof of Mr O'Connell's reckless indifference an issue raised in the appeal? The
parties filed submissions addressing those issues.

The first issue was whether, as Mr O'Connell contended and the DPP

ordering a new trial for the offence of manslaughter (or ordering the entry of a

that effect despite the Court of Appeal entering a verdict of not guilty to murder in
the first judgment. The second ground of Mr O'Connell's appeal to this Court
challenges that holding.

34

The second issue was whether, as the DPP contended and Mr O'Connell
denied, the Court of Appeal had power under s 37O(1)(d) of the SCA to order that
a verdict of guilty of manslaughter be entered. All members of the Court of Appeal
held that the Court had that power and that its exercise was governed by the same
principles as apply to the entry of an alternative verdict under the appeal provisions
of other jurisdictions, 23 including those stated in Spies v The Queen. 24 As part of
his first ground of appeal in this Court, Mr O'Connell contends that s 37O(1)(d)

No analogy with Baden-Clay

44

No relevant analogy can be drawn between the approach taken by the
majority of the Court of Appeal in the first judgment, in considering whether the
evidence proved that Mr O'Connell was recklessly indifferent to the probability of

Ms Jordan's death, and the approach taken by the Court of Appeal of the Supreme
Court of Queensland in R v Baden-Clay that was rejected in this Court.
45

In Baden-Clay, the respondent was convicted of the murder of his wife. At
his trial the respondent gave evidence denying that he had anything to do with his
wife's death or the disposal of her body. 34 The Court of Appeal upheld a ground of
appeal contending that the murder conviction was unreasonable, and substituted a
verdict of guilty to manslaughter. 35 The Court of Appeal concluded that the
prosecution had not disproved a reasonable hypothesis that the respondent was
guilty of manslaughter but not guilty of murder, namely, that in a physical
confrontation with his wife, the respondent "delivered a blow which killed her (for
example, by the effects of a fall hitting her head against a hard surface) without
intending to cause serious harm; and, in a state of panic and knowing that he had
unlawfully killed her, he took her body to Kholo Creek in the hope that it would
be washed away, while lying about the causes of the marks on his face which
suggested conflict". 36

46

In Baden-Clay, this Court held that, in circumstances where there was no
evidence led at the trial that suggested that the respondent killed his wife in a
physical confrontation without intending to do so, the hypothesis identified by the
Court of Appeal was not based on the evidence but was mere speculation or
conjecture. 37 Further, this Court held that even though the jury had rejected the
respondent's evidence, the fact that his evidence was inconsistent with the
hypothesis identified by the Court of Appeal was still of significance because "it
was open to the jury to consider that the hypothesis identified by the Court of
Appeal was not a reasonable inference from the evidence when the only witness
who could have given evidence to support the hypothesis gave evidence which
necessarily excluded it as a possibility". 38

47

different from that contended for by the prosecution and the accused (and

Disposition of the DPP's appeal
71

Having found that the majority of the Court of Appeal erred in upholding
Mr O'Connell's unreasonable verdict ground, the question arises as to whether this
Court should determine that ground or remit the proceedings to the Court of Appeal
to be redetermined in accordance with this Court's reasons.

72

Relying on Pell v The Queen, 66 the DPP contended that if error on the part
of the majority of the Court of Appeal was demonstrated, this Court should proceed
to pronounce the judgment or order which the Court of Appeal should have
pronounced or made. The DPP submitted that the parties had a full opportunity to
place sufficient material before the Court to enable the grounds to be determined
and the orders the DPP sought to be made, and that, in any event, all relevant
material was before the Court.

73

Mr O'Connell denied that the grant of special leave extended to arguing the
outcome of his appeal and contended that the appeal only concerned alleged error
in the approach of the majority of the Court of Appeal. He also submitted that the
Court was not in a position to assess the whole of the evidence, pointing to the
absence in the materials before this Court of the CCTV footage, including
screenshots, and footage of Ms X taken by the police at the scene during which
Ms X was said to be "showing the police officer various things that she says
happened" by "pointing and gesturing to different things". Watching that footage
was said to enable a court to see "the particular things that [Ms X] is referring to,
directions and movements on the bonnet of the car and so on".

74

In circumstances where it is found that an intermediate appellate court has
erred in determining an unreasonable verdict ground, whether this Court will
determine that ground for itself or remit the proceedings to the appellate court will
depend on an assessment of various factors that inform what the "interests of
justice" require. 67 Those factors include the nature of the appellate court's error,
what the particular case requires in reviewing the record of the trial in accordance

another verdict)" redundant. The ordinary meaning of "another verdict" in this
context is a verdict different from "not guilty" and plainly includes a verdict of
guilty. Provisions granting powers to or conferring jurisdiction upon a court such
as s 37O should not be construed as subject to limitations that are not apparent on
their terms. 92

before the jury. If the Court of Appeal sets aside the conviction for the offence

addressed by the Court of Appeal either by entering a verdict for the alternative

Although an answer "Yes" to the third question would plainly have been in
the interests of Mr O'Connell, it appears that the reason that counsel for
Mr O'Connell had urged the trial judge not to answer the question was that counsel
was labouring under the misconception, arising from a decision of the Court of
Criminal Appeal of New South Wales in 2002 122 based upon a different provision
(concerned with "reckless indifference to human life", not "reckless indifference
to the probability of causing the death of any person"), that a probability of death
was a "substantial or real chance as distinct from a mere possibility".

109

Despite the use of the expression "substantial chance" when dealing with
the flexible concept of a "likely" consequence, 123 it might be seriously doubted
whether, in the context of a test for "reckless indifference to human life", the Court
of Criminal Appeal was correct to equate a requirement for a "probable result"
with a "substantial or real chance as distinct from a mere possibility". 124 But
whether or not the Court of Criminal Appeal was correct, that expression of a
substantial or real chance should not be transplanted to dilute the terms of
s 12(1)(b) of the Crimes Act.

110
