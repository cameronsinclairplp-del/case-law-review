# Brawn v The King [2025] HCA 20

_Held for review — the fact-check found problems with this write-up (pipeline/audit.py, run by the daily bot and by add_case.py --audit); treat every statement below as unverified until the hold is cleared._

## One line
High Court sets the test for when a trial error or irregularity — here a breach of the prosecution's disclosure duty — amounts to a miscarriage of justice: it must be material, meaning it could realistically have affected the jury's reasoning to guilt.

## What happened
BRAWN was convicted after a District Court of South Australia jury trial of maintaining an unlawful sexual relationship with a child under 17 (Criminal Law Consolidation Act 1935 (SA), s 50(1)). The complainant, a girl aged between 5 and 8 during the offence period (indictment period 01/04/2016 to 01/01/2019), had been abused. The principal issue at trial was identity — whether BRAWN or another adult male in the complainant's Sudanese community was the perpetrator. The complainant, like other children, referred to elder males, including BRAWN's father 'X', as 'uncle', and she had told a school support worker her 'uncle' abused her. The defence case suggested 'some other uncle' was the real perpetrator but did not nominate X specifically. After BRAWN was sentenced on 04/08/2021, the prosecution disclosed that X had himself been charged in July 2019 with six sexual offences against a teenage niece ('Y') committed November 2018 to May 2019 — a period overlapping the indictment. Those charges were discontinued in August 2020 before arraignment. This had not been disclosed to the defence before or during trial.

## What the Court held
The Court of Appeal found the prosecution breached its common law duty of disclosure but held there was no miscarriage of justice because BRAWN had not shown his defence 'would' or 'might' have been conducted differently. The High Court held that was the wrong test. Where there is a non-fundamental error or irregularity in a trial, to establish a miscarriage of justice under the third limb of the common form appeal provision it must be shown the error was material — that it could realistically have affected the reasoning of the jury to a verdict of guilty. 'Could' means 'having the capacity to'; 'realistically' distinguishes a real possibility from one that is fanciful or improbable. The appellant bears this burden, but it is not onerous, and it is not a counterfactual inquiry into what defence counsel would have done or whether the accused would have been convicted anyway. The materiality inquiry looks at the possible effect on the trial that was actually had, and does not collapse into the proviso. A fundamental error automatically establishes a substantial miscarriage. The proviso is a separate step, the burden of which rests on the prosecution. Here the non-disclosure denied BRAWN the opportunity, or at least an enhanced opportunity, to point to X as a possible perpetrator, and that could realistically have affected the jury's reasoning. Miscarriage of justice established.

## What it means for your casework
This confirms and clarifies what it means for your casework around the prosecution's duty of disclosure. The prosecution — treated as including the police, not just the ODPP — must disclose all relevant evidence, and the duty extends to material of forensic utility even if only usable indirectly, such as material informing a line of inquiry, identifying witnesses, or assisting cross-examination. Here the knowledge sat with police and that was enough to fix the prosecution with the duty; the individual prosecutor's ignorance did not excuse the breach. If you become aware of charges or allegations against a third party who could be an alternative suspect, that is disclosable, and failure to disclose can quash a conviction and force a retrial. Do not assume material is irrelevant just because the accused already knew of it or the defence did not specifically pursue it. Practically: keep disclosure obligations live throughout a matter, flag any third-party suspect material to the ODPP early, and document your disclosure decisions. The materiality test is a low bar for defendants — a lost forensic opportunity that could realistically have shifted the jury's reasoning is enough.

## Verdict
AWARENESS-plus for disclosure practice — it reshapes appellate doctrine but its practical bite for you is the reminder that police-held third-party suspect material is disclosable and that non-disclosure can sink a conviction.

## Flags (verify before relying)
- The offence provision (s 50(1)) is from the <i>Criminal Law Consolidation Act 1935</i> (SA); the appeal provision (s 158) is from the <i>Criminal Procedure Act 1921</i> (SA) — both South Australian, not WA. The judgment notes the WA equivalent common form provision is <i>Criminal Appeals Act 2004</i> (WA), s 30(3).
- The judgment is a South Australian matter; the disclosure and materiality principles are High Court doctrine of general application, but the specific statutory framework differs in WA.
- Ground 2 (the alleged concession) was not decided because the appeal succeeded on Ground 1; the Court expressly declined to confirm the scope of disclosure was fully discharged.

2

The appellant appealed his conviction. The Court of Appeal of the Supreme
Court of South Australia found that the prosecution breached its common law duty
of disclosure by failing to disclose to the appellant and his legal representatives
that X had been charged with committing unlawful sexual acts against a teenage
girl during a period that overlapped with the period of the indictment. However,
the Court of Appeal held that there was no miscarriage of justice because it had
not been demonstrated that, had that disclosure been made prior to or during the
trial, the appellant's defence either "would" or "might" have been conducted
differently.3

3

For the reasons that follow, where it has been shown that there was error or
irregularity in a criminal trial, such as the breach of the prosecution's duty of
disclosure that occurred in this case, then to establish a miscarriage of justice it
must be shown that the error or irregularity was material in the sense that the error
or irregularity could realistically have affected the reasoning of the jury to its
verdict. The Court of Appeal erred to the extent that it required that it be
demonstrated that the appellant's defence would have been conducted differently
but for the error or irregularity and erred in failing to conclude that a miscarriage
of justice was demonstrated. Accordingly, the appeal should be allowed, the
appellant's conviction set aside and a new trial ordered.

For other errors or irregularities to constitute a miscarriage of justice, they
must be material in the sense that the error or irregularity could realistically have
affected the reasoning of the jury to a verdict of guilty that was returned by the
jury in the criminal trial that occurred. In this context, "could" is to be understood
as meaning "having the capacity to", and "realistically" distinguishes the relevant
assessment of the possibility of a different outcome from a possibility that is
fanciful or improbable. This threshold to establish that an error or irregularity is
material must be satisfied by the appellant, but that burden is not onerous. It does
not invite an analysis of whether, but for the error, the accused might or might not
have been found guilty.

11

However, as explained, the test for whether an error or irregularity is

affected the reasoning of the jury to the verdict of guilty.53
