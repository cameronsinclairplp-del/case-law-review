# Rose v The State of Western Australia [2026] WASCA 123

## One line
Court of Appeal refuses a remand prisoner's application for orders directing the prison on how to manage his custody so he can prepare a bail appeal: its power to give such directions is 'highly doubtful' and, even if it existed, this was not a case for it.

## What happened
Andrew Bo ROSE was serving a 6 year 4 month sentence (backdated to 04/10/2019) for serious offences and was released on parole in July 2024. On 26/03/2025, still on parole, he was charged with two counts of aggravated burglary and two counts of possession of prohibited drugs with intent to sell or supply — all serious offences under sch 2 to the Bail Act. His parole was revoked and he was remanded at Bunbury Regional Prison, with trial listed in the Magistrates Court for 27/10/2026. His third bail application was refused by HOWARD J on 15/05/2026. ROSE appealed under s 15A of the Bail Act and, acting in person, applied for interim orders directing the prison to give him a cell computer, free copying/printing, facilitated calls to businesses to find witnesses, in-cell law books, careful handling of evidential mail, and placement in a prison with an open library. He had not filed a compliant appellant's case.

## What the Court held
The Application for interim orders was dismissed and an extension of time to file the appellant's case was granted. The Court held it is highly doubtful the court has any power to direct prison authorities on how a prison or prisoner is managed in order to preserve its appellate jurisdiction — the CEO of the Department of Justice is responsible for prison management under s 7 of the Prisons Act 1981 (WA) and is not a party to the appeal. Adopting the NSW reasoning in Liristis and Hamzy, the Court held its role is to ensure a fair trial and at most an 'irreducible minimum' of resources, not equality of arms, with corrections authorities better placed to weigh security. Even if the power existed, this was not an appropriate case: ROSE was disadvantaged but not denied access to the appellate jurisdiction. The Court also stressed that under s 15B(2) the appeal is confined to the material before the primary judge, so seeking new evidence for the bail appeal had no utility, and that new facts should instead found a fresh s 14(2a) application.

## What it means for your casework
This is a bail/procedural appeal and does not change how you investigate or charge. It is useful background on the custody framework: a person charged with a sch 2 serious offence while on parole faces the exceptional reasons test for bail (cl 3A, pt C, sch 1), and repeat bail applications require new facts or inadequate earlier presentation under s 14(2a). Practically, it confirms that a remand prisoner's complaints about difficulty preparing a defence in custody are managed by corrections and the trial court, not by orders against police or prisons via the appeal court. If a self-represented accused complains to you about disclosure or preparation, note that adequacy of disclosure is a matter for the trial court (here the Magistrates Court), and the prison controls access to computers, copying and legal resources.

## Verdict
AWARENESS only — a procedural bail-appeal decision about the court's power over prison management; it does not affect investigative steps, evidence gathering or charging.

19

The conferral of jurisdiction on the court carries with it implicit
power to make orders, at least against the parties to the proceeding
against whom final relief might be granted, as are necessary to ensure
the effective exercise of its jurisdiction.3 That general principle applies

to the conferral of appellate jurisdiction on this court by s 15A of the
Bail Act.
20

It has been held that, where proceedings are pending in this court
between the parties to an appeal, the Court of Appeal division may
exercise the court's power to make such procedural directions in those
proceedings as are necessary for the purpose of preserving the integrity
of the exercise of the court's appellate jurisdiction.4 However, we are
not aware of any case where the court has held that its implicit powers
to preserve the effective exercise of its appellate jurisdiction extend to
making orders directed to prison authorities as to how prisons or
prisoners are to be managed.

21

Under s 7 of the Prisons Act 1981 (WA), the Chief Executive
Officer of the Department of Justice is responsible for the management,
control, and security of all prisons and the welfare and safe custody of
all prisoners, subject to that Act and the control of the Minister. The
Chief Executive Officer is not a party to this appeal. There is no clear
basis on which this court would have power to issue directions to the
prison authorities as to the management of prisons or prisoners for the
purposes of preserving the effective exercise of its appellate
jurisdiction.

22

In our view the following observations of Basten JA (Beazley P
agreeing) in Commissioner of Corrective Services (NSW) v Liristis (a
case concerned with the Supreme Court's power to give directions to
facilitate an accused's preparation for trial) are apposite:5
The proposition that the Supreme Court has power to order a gaoler to
take steps to provide a prisoner with resources to run his criminal
defence goes a step further than any authority to which the court was
directed and is untenable. Importantly, no authority suggested that it
was a proper function of the Supreme Court to weigh the demands of a
prisoner for access to equipment which was not permitted in the gaol
according to ordinary operating procedures against the gaoler's concern
for security.
It is notorious that security in prisons is a serious issue at all times. The
particular concerns raised in this case involved the potential for
smuggling in small modems which could then be used by prisoners with
mobile phones (illegal, but known to exist within the gaols) to obtain
access to the internet (also a prohibited practice).

By contrast, where a prisoner seeks the taking of steps necessary to
ensure a fair trial, the nature of the claim must be analysed in the
context of the system of criminal justice. It is undeniable that, at least
in many cases, a person who is arrested, charged and denied bail will be
hindered or impeded by the constraints of custody, as compared with an
accused person who is at large in the community awaiting trial. The
accused person in custody may apply for bail, but has no separate
entitlement to seek an order from a Supreme Court judge directing that
he or she be released from custody by the governor of a prison for the
purpose of conferences with his or her legal representatives. The
assumption underlying the significant number of cases addressing
communications between prisoners and their legal representatives is
that such communications must occur within the confines of detention.
23

The approach of Basten JA in Liristis was approved by Bell CJ
(Payne and Stern JJA agreeing) in Commissioner of Corrective
Services (NSW) v Hamzy.6 That case raised the question of whether
the court could make orders against prison authorities to make facilities
available to a prisoner taking civil proceedings against prison
authorities. The court held that the primary judge in Hamzy erred in
finding that the court had power to make directions to achieve an
'equality of arms'. Bell CJ observed:
The role of the Court is to ensure a fair trial and it may be accepted that
an 'irreducible minimum' of resources may be required to achieve that
but that, too, is different from an equalisation or an approximate
equalisation of resources.
Further, any irreducible minimum
requirement must be assessed in the context of the particular case,
including the applicable statutory context which confers discretions and
power on corrections authorities who are far better placed than courts to
assess what is required for the safe and secure management of
corrections facilities.
