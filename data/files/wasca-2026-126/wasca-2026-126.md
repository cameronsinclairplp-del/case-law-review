# Cooper v The State of Western Australia [2026] WASCA 126

## One line
Court of Appeal upholds a co-offender's sentence, confirming that a 'mastermind' and a 'willing participant' can be equally culpable for what happened at the scene despite different roles beforehand.

## What happened
On 25/05/2018, five men — Paul BONNER (the 'mastermind'), the appellant James COOPER, Shannon BONNER, Waylon KELLS and Darryl PEARSON — carried out a premeditated vigilante home invasion in Kununurra as revenge for a perceived wrong against Paul BONNER's son. In the weeks before, Paul BONNER and KELLS phoned the victim Amanda QUARTERMAINE and threatened her and her partner Corbin CLYDEN. Paul BONNER and KELLS travelled to Kununurra from Shepparton, Victoria; Shannon BONNER recruited COOPER and the two travelled together from Darwin, each using stolen numberplates on their vehicles to avoid detection at state borders; the attack was staged from PEARSON's Kununurra workshop. Shortly after 3.50am, masked and dressed in dark clothing, they kicked in the locked front door and bedroom door while the couple and four children slept. At least three men struck CLYDEN with poles to the head, body, arms and legs; QUARTERMAINE was struck on the arm. CLYDEN needed nine sutures to his scalp. After a nine-day trial, all five were convicted of aggravated home burglary with intent (s 401(1)(a)), assault occasioning bodily harm (s 317(1)), and doing an act likely to endanger life, health or safety (s 304(1)(b)). COOPER was sentenced to a total effective 6 years 8 months; Paul BONNER to 7 years 6 months. COOPER appealed on the sole ground that the 10-month gap was too small and breached the parity principle.

## What the Court held
The appeal was dismissed and leave refused. The Court held the 10-month difference between the sentences did not offend the parity principle and would not engender a legitimate or justifiable sense of grievance in an objective observer. Although Paul BONNER was the mastermind who made the threats, had the motive, was the oldest and had a significant record for violent offending, the sentencing judge found each co-offender equally culpable for the offending at the house because each acted pursuant to a common purpose and the assaults were a probable consequence of that plan. The Court accepted the finding that COOPER was not 'roped in' but a willing and enthusiastic participant — travelling from Darwin with 48 hours to reconsider. The distinction in roles existed only before the offence; on the day itself there was no difference. The 10 months adequately reflected BONNER's mastermind role, his violent antecedents and COOPER's youth. The Court also stressed that at age 25 youth carries only little weight as a mitigating factor.

## What it means for your casework
This is chiefly an awareness case, but it reinforces evidential themes useful to your casework. First, common purpose (probable consequence) means every participant can be held equally culpable for the violence at the scene, even where you cannot prove which individual struck the blows — the judge here could not identify who hit whom, yet all five were convicted. Build the case around the shared plan, not just who wielded the weapon. Second, physical evidence tying a suspect to preparatory acts is powerful: COOPER's fingerprint was found on the stolen numberplate fitted to the vehicle he and Shannon BONNER drove from Darwin, and the Court treated that trip — with some 48 hours to reconsider — as showing a willing and enthusiastic participant, not a man 'roped in'. Preserve and forensically examine vehicles, plates and staging locations. Third, the pre-offence threats, cross-border travel, disguises and stolen plates all evidenced premeditation and consciousness of guilt — document the full timeline, not just the assault. Fourth, an offender aged 25 or over will get little sentencing benefit from 'youth', so don't overstate that concern when assessing a suspect's likely outcome.

## Verdict
AWARENESS only — this is a sentence-parity appeal that does not change investigative or charging rules, but it usefully reaffirms common-purpose culpability and the value of forensic links to preparatory acts.

Infringement of the parity principle - relevant legal principles
53

The principles to be applied in determining whether this court
should interfere with a sentence on parity grounds are well established.
In Ngo v The Queen13 Buss P (Mazza JA agreeing) summarised those
principles as follows:
36

The object of the parity principle is to ensure appropriate
consistency in the sentencing of co-offenders. The critical
question is whether disparity or lack of disparity in the
sentencing outcome is capable of giving rise to a legitimate or
justifiable sense of grievance, or to give the appearance in the
mind of an objective observer that justice has not been done.
The applicable test is objective not subjective. The application
and effect of relevant sentencing principles must be taken into
account in determining whether there is a legitimate or
justifiable sense of grievance.

37

An appellate court may interfere, on the ground of a marked and
unjustifiable disparity or on the ground of an absence of a
marked and justifiable disparity, with a sentencing judge's
exercise of the sentencing discretion even though the sentence in
question, viewed in isolation, would not necessarily be regarded
as manifestly excessive or otherwise open to challenge. But
parity of sentencing does not require a sentencing judge to be so
lenient as to 'shock the public conscience' by imposing a
sentence entirely disproportionate to the offence in question.

38

Her Honour expressly had regard to the fact that Paul Bonner was
the mastermind of the plan to seek retribution for Harland Bonner and
that he made threats of violence to Ms Quartermaine on 12 May 2018,
13 days before the offences were committed. However, there was no
evidence led at trial capable of supporting a finding that the appellant
had been 'roped in' to the offending or that Paul Bonner was a person of
authority in relation to, or had influence over, the appellant. To the
contrary, her Honour found that the appellant was a willing participant
in carrying out the plan instigated by Paul Bonner. The fact that the
appellant travelled with Shannon Bonner from Darwin to Kununurra is
illustrative of his enthusiasm to be part of the group that intended to
commit these offences. The appellant had ample time, in the order of
48 hours, to reconsider his participation in this offending.

68

On the basis of the unchallenged factual findings of the sentencing
judge, while there was a difference in the roles that the appellant and
Paul Bonner had prior to the offending on 25 May 2018, there was no
difference in their respective roles in their serious offending on that
date.

69

The offending in this case was extremely serious - five masked
men carrying weapons forcefully invaded a home in the early hours of
the morning, while the occupants, including four children, were
sleeping. Three of those men then assaulted two victims by striking
them with the weapons. This was a premeditated vigilante attack, which
terrified the victims. Paul Bonner and the appellant were equally
culpable for the offending on 25 May 2018. While Paul Bonner was the
mastermind behind that offending, the appellant was a willing and
enthusiastic participant.

70

Personal deterrence was a relevant sentencing factor. Her Honour
recognised that Paul Bonner's criminal record for violent offending,
particularly for a violent offence that bore a similarity to this offending,
meant that there was a 'strong' need for personal deterrence in his
case.21 While the appellant's antecedents were less serious than those of
Paul Bonner, the fact that he had a prior conviction for carrying a
weapon - and given weapons were carried in this offending - meant that
personal deterrence was still a relevant sentencing factor.

72

In our view, the appellant's youth was not a significant mitigating
factor. This court has held that where an offender is 25 years old at the
age of committing an offence, youth can only be afforded little
weight.22

73

Determining the appropriate distinction to be made between
offenders to reflect the difference between them is a matter of
discretion. In our view, the difference in the total effective sentences
between the appellant and Paul Bonner, being 10 months less for the
appellant than for Paul Bonner, was properly open to her Honour to
capture the differences in the criminality and involvement of each of
them, the relative youth of the appellant compared to Paul Bonner and
Paul Bonner's antecedents compared to those of the appellant. We are
satisfied the difference of 10 months between the two sentences did not
infringe the parity principle or the principle of equal justice.

74

Whilst the appellant may have a subjective sense of grievance over
the lack of sufficient disparity between his sentence and that of Paul
Bonner, it has not been demonstrated that the difference in total
effective sentences imposed upon the appellant and Paul Bonner is such
as to engender a legitimate or justifiable sense of grievance on the
appellant's part or to give the appearance in the mind of an objective
observer that justice was not done as between the appellant and Paul
Bonner.
