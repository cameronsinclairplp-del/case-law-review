# Potter (A Pseudonym) v The King [2026] HCA 25

## One line
High Court held a wife's covert recording of her husband admitting to raping her while she slept was <b>reasonably necessary to protect her lawful interests</b> and so was lawfully made and admissible.

## What happened
POTTER (a pseudonym) stood trial in the District Court of SA on four counts of rape against his wife, the allegation being that he had sex with her without consent while she was asleep. He was convicted on counts 2 and 4. Before count 4 occurred, on 14/12/2019 the complainant used her mobile phone to covertly record a conversation in which POTTER admitted "I sexually assaulted you" and "I started having sex with you when you were asleep". Her stated reason was to remind herself never to return to him. The trial judge admitted the recording; the SA Court of Appeal split, with the majority (S Doyle and David JJA) holding the statutory exception was not met but wrongly admitting it as a discretion to admit unlawfully obtained evidence, while Kourakis CJ held the exception was met. POTTER appealed to the High Court.

## What the Court held
The High Court (five Justices, unanimous) dismissed the appeal, upholding the Crown's notice of contention. It held the making of the recording was reasonably necessary for the protection of the complainant's lawful interests under s 4(2)(a)(ii) of the Surveillance Devices Act 2016 (SA), so it was not prohibited by s 4(1) and was properly admitted. Key points: "lawful interests" is wider than legal rights, title, duty or liability and is akin to legitimate interests, applied case-by-case. "Reasonably necessary" does not mean "essential" — it is enough that the use was appropriate to protect the interest; a proportionality/"reasonably appropriate and adapted" test was rejected. The assessment is objective but judged on the circumstances the recording party faced at the time, with the person's reasons informing but not determining the analysis. The relevant lawful interest was correctly characterised as the complainant's right to bodily autonomy and integrity and not being raped — an interest deserving "the very strongest protection" — not merely her interest in ending the relationship. Given she was vulnerable, still living with POTTER, had been raped repeatedly, the recording was an appropriate powerful reminder of the peril she faced. The Court also confirmed that once the exception is not met, there is no discretion to admit such evidence — the SDA excludes it (s 12(1)).

## What it means for your casework
This is a South Australian statute, so it does not bind you directly — WA is governed by the Surveillance Devices Act 1998 (WA), which has its own s 5(3)(d) "protection of lawful interests" exception (and, unlike SA, generally requires a principal party's consent). Treat this as strong persuasive authority on how courts read the "reasonably necessary for the protection of lawful interests" exception. For your casework it means a complainant's covert recording of an offender's admissions may be lawfully made and admissible where it protects a serious interest such as bodily integrity or safety from further offending — even if the complainant made it only as a "note to herself" and not for a prosecution. The test is appropriate, not essential: the fact the complainant could have gone to police, or made a written note instead, does not defeat it. When you receive a victim's covert recording, gather evidence of why and when it was made and the circumstances they faced, because that drives admissibility. Remember the mirror risk: recordings made to trap, pressure or gain leverage (rather than to protect an interest) will not qualify. Also note that if the exception is not satisfied there is no discretion to save the recording — confirm the WA equivalent before relying on any covert recording.

## Verdict
AWARENESS-level: this interprets SA legislation, not WA law, but gives authoritative High Court guidance on the "reasonably necessary for protection of lawful interests" test that WA courts will find persuasive when assessing covert recordings.

The Court of Appeal's reasons

31

The appellant applied for permission to appeal against his convictions on
counts 2 and 4.22 The relevant ground of his application contended that the trial
judge erred in admitting the covert recording. The appellant contended that the
recording was unlawful because it was made in contravention of s 4(1) of the SDA,
and that the trial judge erred in concluding that the recording was not unlawful on
the basis that the recording was reasonably necessary for the protection of the
lawful interests of the complainant under s 4(2)(a)(ii) of that Act. The Court of
Appeal granted the appellant permission to appeal against his convictions but
dismissed the appeal.

32

In the Court of Appeal, S Doyle and David JJA rejected the trial judge's
conclusion that the complainant's use of her phone to make the recording was
"reasonably necessary for the protection of [the complainant's] lawful interests".
Their Honours characterised the complainant's purpose in making the recording
not as being to "address any fear or risk of further or ongoing offending", but rather
as being a "reminder to herself not to resume the relationship" with the appellant.
Their Honours observed that "[e]ven accepting that [the complainant] had a lawful
interest in escaping from her relationship with the appellant, and that 'reasonably
necessary' requires only that the recording be reasonably appropriate – as opposed
to essential ... we do not consider that the recording was reasonably necessary for
the protection of the complainant's lawful interests".

33

Thereafter, in a footnote, their Honours observed that, even though the
making of the covert recording did not satisfy s 4(2)(a)(ii), "[b]y reason of s 9(1)(d)
of the [SDA] it would not have been unlawful to use the recording at trial.
