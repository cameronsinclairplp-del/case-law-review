# Le v The King [2026] WASCA 117

_Held for review — the fact-check found problems with this write-up (pipeline/add_case.py --audit); treat every statement below as unverified until the hold is cleared._

## One line
Court of Appeal upholds meth importation convictions, confirming covert recordings, IP tracking and corroborated accomplice evidence together proved a commercial-quantity import agreement.

## What happened
About 20.9 kg (16.8 kg pure) of methamphetamine was concealed in an industrial oven consigned by FedEx from Toronto on 22/12/2018, addressed to "Minh" of a fake kitchen-equipment business (HKM) at a Maddington premises. Federal police intercepted the parcel in Sydney on 03/01/2019, replaced the drug with an inert substance and ran a controlled delivery on 10/01/2019 (no one attended). Minh and the two appellants, Phuoc Sang LE and Thanh Hung NGUYEN (Hung), were arrested on 15/01/2019 and charged under s 307.1(1) of the Criminal Code (Cth). Minh pleaded guilty and testified for a substantial sentence reduction. Both appellants were convicted after a trial that ran from 06/03/2024 to 24/05/2024; Hung was also convicted of failing to comply with a data access order (s 3LA(5) Crimes Act 1914 (Cth)). The prosecution case rested on Minh's accomplice evidence, translated telecommunications intercepts and a listening/tracking device in Hung's car (obtained under TI and surveillance device warrants), FedEx tracking traced to LE's home router/IP address, and items found on search. Both appellants gave evidence denying knowledge — Hung claimed the venture was tobacco/gemstones, LE claimed an ordinary commercial lease and a Ghana gold business.

## What the Court held
Hung's appeal was dismissed (leave refused) and Sang Le's application for an extension of time was refused, so his appeal stayed dismissed under the springing order. On its own review of the record the Court found only one reasonable inference open on the evidence as a whole — an agreement to import a commercial quantity of methamphetamine — and called the prosecution case strong and compelling. It may not have been open to convict on Minh's evidence alone, but his account was corroborated by the covert recordings, Hung's movements, the seized items and the FedEx tracking. The covert recordings were admissible against both appellants — as circumstantial evidence of the agreement and, where its requirements were met, as acts or declarations in furtherance under the co-conspirator exception to the hearsay rule. The interpreter was qualified by a combination of study and experience to translate Vietnamese and a childhood rhyming slang; the concession that his evidence was admissible was correctly made. The Court did not decide whether the prosecutor's questions implying the accused should have called corroborating witnesses were improper: the respondent conceded they should not have been asked, and on that assumption the trial judge's clear, repeated no-onus directions meant no miscarriage of justice, so refusing to discharge the jury was plainly correct. The expert-evidence direction was orthodox. Leave to adduce additional evidence was refused: for Hung it was new, not fresh, evidence; for Sang Le the Court left open whether it was fresh, because either way there was no significant possibility of an acquittal.

## What it means for your casework
For your casework this confirms several practical points. Corroborate accomplice/informer evidence — the Court doubted a conviction on Minh's evidence alone would have been open, so build independent support (intercepts, surveillance, financial and search material). Covert product from properly warranted TI and surveillance devices can run against every party to an agreement, not just the speaker, via the co-conspirator exception — capture the whole picture, because forensic force comes from the totality, not one disputed phrase. Get properly accredited interpreters for covert recordings; capability by study and experience covers dialect/slang, and disputes over a few words go to weight. Digital tracing matters — IP/router attribution (here FedEx web-tracking tied to LE's home router) is strong circumstantial evidence; corroborate with device location and surveillance timing. For data access orders (s 3LA), the live issue is proving the suspect was capable of complying (knew the PIN/password) — gather evidence linking the person to actual use of the device. Finally, never let questioning imply the accused must call evidence or prove anything — the onus stays on the prosecution.

## Verdict
AWARENESS — an appellate application of settled principles; the practice points on corroborating accomplice evidence, warranted covert product running against every party to the agreement, interpreter qualification and s 3LA capability reinforce rather than change how you work.

established. The principles were recently summarised in Visser v

6

1.

It is a question of fact whether, having regard to the evidence, a
verdict of guilty on which a conviction is based is unreasonable
or cannot be supported.

2.

An appellate court must decide that question by making its own
independent assessment of the sufficiency and quality of the
evidence, and determining whether, notwithstanding that there is
evidence upon which a tribunal of fact might convict,
nevertheless it would be dangerous in all the circumstances to
permit the verdict to stand.

3.

The appellate court, in making an independent assessment of the
whole of the evidence to determine whether it was open to the
tribunal of fact to be satisfied beyond reasonable doubt as to the
guilt of the accused, must weigh the whole of the evidence (in
particular, the competing evidence).

4.

The appellate court's task is not to consider, as a question of law,
merely whether there was sufficient evidence to sustain a
conviction.

5.

The appellate court, in assessing whether it was open to the
tribunal of fact to be satisfied beyond reasonable doubt as to the
guilt of the accused, must not disregard or discount either the
consideration that the jury is the body entrusted with the primary
responsibility of determining guilt or innocence or the
consideration that the jury has had the benefit of having seen and
heard the witnesses. On the contrary, the court must pay full
regard to those considerations.

6.

The appellate court's function is to determine for itself whether
the evidence at trial was sufficient in nature and quality to remove
any reasonable doubt that the accused was guilty of the offence
charged. The critical issue is whether the court's assessment of
the totality of the evidence leaves the court with a reasonable
doubt as to guilt, which the court cannot assuage by having regard
to such advantage as the jury can be taken to have had by reason
of having seen and heard the evidence at trial.

7.

The appellate court examines the trial record to ascertain whether,
despite the jury's advantage in having seen and heard the
witnesses in the context of the trial, the jury, acting rationally,
should have entertained a reasonable doubt as to proof of guilt.

evidence establishes that the appellant is innocent or raises such a doubt

There is no evidence as to whether Sang Le was aware of Hung's
