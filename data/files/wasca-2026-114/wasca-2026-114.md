# Frigger v The State of Western Australia [2026] WASCA 114

## One line
A second appeal against a s 169 perjury conviction failed where the proposed 'fresh/new and compelling evidence' was neither fresh nor compelling and had no reasonable prospect of establishing innocence.

## What happened
On 04/06/2024 FRIGGER was convicted after a jury trial of one count of knowingly making a false statement under oath in a material particular, contrary to s 169 of the Criminal Code (WA). The charge arose from two irreconcilable affidavits she swore (in 2008 and 2010) in civil proceedings involving PSA, Mr BANNING, CAT and Mr KITAY — one of the two statements had to be false. Her first appeal was dismissed on 14/01/2025. On 02/06/2026 she brought a second appeal under pt 3A of the Criminal Appeals Act 2004 (WA), relying on an affidavit and seeking a witness summons to Mr KITAY to produce a 2010 'reconstructed Directors' Loan Account'. She argued (1) the civil proceedings were a nullity because PSA lacked corporate authority to bring them, so no statement could be 'material', and (2) new evidence contradicted the prosecution case about directors' fees.

## What the Court held
Leave to appeal was refused, the witness-summons application dismissed and the appeal dismissed. The court held the ground identified no fresh and compelling or new and compelling evidence and, in any event, had no reasonable prospect of succeeding. On particular 1, the 'nullity' argument was not new — it had been run in related litigation before trial and could have been raised with reasonable diligence; deemed civil admissions did not bind the State in the criminal case; superior court orders are valid until set aside so the proceedings were not nullities; and the affidavits were in fact relied on and so were capable of affecting the outcome, making them false in a material particular. On particular 2, evidence of a 2010 reconciliation showing FRIGGER a debtor rather than creditor was not compelling — the prosecution never sought to prove which of the two affidavits was false, only that they were irreconcilable so one must be — and evidence of the Freehills advice was in her possession before trial and remained subject to privilege/confidentiality orders. The court confirmed the prosecutor's disclosure duty is to disclose material in the possession of the investigators, not an obligation to go out and obtain material.

## What it means for your casework
This is mainly background awareness, but note two practical points. First, on s 169 perjury: a statement is false 'in a material particular' if it is reasonably capable of affecting the outcome of the purpose for which it was made — you do not need to prove the proceedings would have turned out differently, only that the statement could have affected them. Where a person swears two irreconcilable sworn statements, the prosecution can succeed by proving both cannot be true (so one must be false) without pinning down which one. Second, on disclosure: your duty runs to evidentiary material in the possession of the investigating agency. A defence complaint that the DPP failed to go and obtain some external document does not create a disclosure breach — but you should make sure everything the investigation actually holds is captured and passed on.

## Verdict
AWARENESS — it confirms how materiality under s 169 works and the scope of the disclosure duty, but does not change day-to-day investigative practice.

Legal framework
9

10

Under s 35E of the CA Act, an offender convicted of an offence
on indictment may bring a second or subsequent appeal against
conviction if:
(a)

there is fresh and compelling evidence relating to the offence; or

(b)

there is new and compelling evidence relating to the offence.

For these purposes, evidence is relevantly 'fresh' if, despite the
exercise of reasonable diligence, the evidence was not and could not
have been tendered at the trial of the offence or any previous appeal.
Evidence not tendered at trial or a previous appeal is 'new' if it could,
with the exercise of reasonable diligence, have been tendered at the trial
of the offence or any previous appeal. Evidence will be compelling

[2026] WASCA 114
REASONS OF THE COURT

only if it is highly probative in the context of the issues in dispute at the
appellant's trial.1
11

Under s 35F of the CA Act, leave to appeal is required for each
ground of appeal in an appeal brought under pt 3A of that Act.
Section 35F(4) provides that this court must not give leave to appeal on
a ground of appeal unless it is satisfied:
(a)

the ground identifies fresh and compelling evidence or new and
compelling evidence that should, in the interests of justice, be
considered on an appeal; and

(b)

the ground has a reasonable prospect of succeeding.

Unless this court grants leave to appeal on at least one ground of
appeal, the appeal is taken to be dismissed.
12

Under s 35H(2) of the CA Act, an appeal based on fresh evidence
must be allowed if the court is satisfied that there was a miscarriage of
justice. Under s 35H(4), an appeal based on new evidence must be
allowed if the court is satisfied on the balance of probabilities that, in
light of all of the evidence, the evidence establishes that the offender is
innocent. Otherwise, under s 35H(1), the appeal must be dismissed.

13

The operation of pt 3A of the CA Act was considered in Luo v
The King.2 As the court observed in that case:3
[U]nder s 35E(1), a second or subsequent appeal may only be brought if
there is fresh and compelling or new and compelling evidence. That is,
fresh and compelling or new and compelling evidence must actually
exist at the time the appeal is brought. The bringing of a second or
subsequent appeal is not a speculative venture. It can only be
competently brought if fresh and compelling or new and compelling
evidence exists at the time the appeal notice is filed. Put another way, a
second or subsequent appeal cannot be competently brought on the
basis that the fresh and compelling or new and compelling evidence
upon which the appeal is purportedly brought might exist, or will be put
before the court later in the course of the appeal. This is not to deny the
general power of the court to admit other evidence in the appeal
pursuant to s 40(1)(e) of the [CA Act], but this power does not entitle
an appellant to bring a second or subsequent appeal without putting
before the court, when the appeal notice is filed, the fresh and
compelling, or new and compelling, evidence. (original emphasis)
