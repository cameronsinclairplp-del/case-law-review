FIANNACA J:
The application and its history
	This is the first review of the respondent's detention under a continuing detention order (CDO) made on 15 March 2024 by Derrick J pursuant to s 48(1)(a) of the High Risk Serious Offenders Act 2020 (WA) (HRSO Act): see The State of Western Australia v Hill [No 2] [2024] WASC 70 (Hill [No 2]). A CDO is an order that an offender be detained in custody for an indefinite term for control, care, or treatment. Derrick J made the order after finding that the respondent was a high risk serious offender, in that it was necessary to make a restriction order (being either a CDO or a supervision order) in relation to the respondent to ensure adequate protection of the community against an unacceptable risk that the respondent will commit a serious offence, as defined in the HRSO Act.
	The respondent has a history of committing stalking offences against family members and females with whom he has been in a relationship, and offences of violence against such females. At times, his offending has occurred in the context of breaching a Violence Restraining Order (VRO). The last of his offences was an offence of manslaughter, being the unlawful killing of his then female partner on 1 August 2012. He was convicted of that offence in 2014 and was sentenced on 28 March 2014 to 11 years' imprisonment, the sentence being backdated to commence on 2 August 2012. The respondent was denied release on parole by the Prisoners Review Board. In July 2023, prior to the end of the respondent's sentence, the State of Western Australia (the applicant) applied for a restriction order under the HRSO Act on the basis that the respondent is a high risk serious offender. The application was heard by Derrick J on various dates in January, February and March 2024.
	In determining, on 15 March 2024, that the respondent was a high risk serious offender, Derrick J relied on the respondent's history of offending, assessments that had been made of the respondent by relevant professionals in the past, statements made by the respondent to such professionals, and the evidence of the courtappointed experts in the hearing before his Honour. Those experts were Professor Natalie Pyszora, a Consultant Forensic Psychiatrist, and Dr Tara Yewers, a Counselling Psychologist.
	Derrick J found that the respondent had a propensity to commit serious offences of the kind I have described, and a pattern of offending. His Honour said in respect of those matters (citation omitted):
A person has a propensity to commit serious offences in the future if they have an inclination, tendency or disposition to commit serious offences generally, or in a particular way, or upon a particular type of victim.
Taking into account the respondent's history of offending and the statements that he has made over the years to various professionals which reveal that he has a total lack of insight into his offending, I am satisfied that he has a propensity (that is, an inclination, tendency or disposition) to commit:
1. 	serious offences comprised of stalking offences against family members and against females with whom he has been in a social or intimate relationship, or with whom he is in a social or intimate relationship; and
2. 	serious offences comprised of violent offences against females with whom he has been in a social or intimate relationship, or with whom he is in a social or intimate relationship.
A 'pattern', as it pertains to behaviour, is a recurrent way of acting by an individual towards a given object or in a given situation.
There are patterns to much of the respondent's offending. One pattern is that the respondent routinely contravenes VROs that have been taken out against him by members of his family. Another pattern is that the respondent commits stalking offences against vulnerable females with whom he has developed an intimate or social relationship. The third pattern is that the respondent commits serious acts of violence against, women, particularly older women, with whom he is, or has been, in an intimate relationship.
	His Honour found that the respondent was a high risk serious offender for the following reasons:
On the basis of the evidence of Professor Pyszora and Dr Yewers and having regard to the offences that the respondent has in the past committed and to my above stated finding in relation to his propensity to commit offences, I am satisfied of the following matters:
1.	The respondent will be at high risk of committing serious offences as defined in the [HRSO Act] comprised of stalking and aggravated stalking contrary to s 338E of the [Criminal Code (WA) (the Code)] against family members and against females with whom he has been in a social or intimate relationship with (sic), or with whom he is in a social or intimate relationship, if he is not subject to a continuing detention order or a supervision order;
2.	The respondent will be at a high risk of committing violent offences that are serious offences as defined in the [HRSO Act] against females with whom he has been in a social or intimate relationship with, or with whom he is in a social or intimate relationship, if he is not subject to a continuing detention order or a supervision order; and
3.	The violent offences that are serious offences as defined in the [HRSO Act] that the respondent will be at high risk of committing if he is not subject to a continuing detention order or a supervision order include unlawfully doing grievous bodily harm with intent to do grievous bodily harm or maim, disfigure or disable contrary to s 294 of the Code, unlawfully doing grievous bodily harm contrary to s 297 of the Code, manslaughter contrary to s 280 of the Code and unlawful assault causing death contrary to s 281 of the Code.
…
I have already stated my finding that the respondent is at high risk of committing serious offences comprised of violent offences and stalking offences if he is not subject to a continuing detention order or a supervision order. On the basis of this finding and having regard to the nature and extent of the physical and/or psychological harm that the respondent will cause to the victim if he commits a serious offence comprised of a violent offence or a serious offence comprised of a stalking offence (even a stalking offence that does not involve repeated conduct over an extended period of time), I am satisfied to a high degree of probability that the risk that the respondent will commit a serious offence is unacceptable and that it is necessary to make a continuing detention order or a supervision order in relation to the respondent to ensure adequate protection of the community against the risk. I am so satisfied notwithstanding that the respondent has already been punished for the offences he has committed. It follows that I find that the respondent is a high risk serious offender.
	For reasons that I will refer to below, when I come to discuss Derrick J's findings in more detail, his Honour concluded that the appropriate restriction order at that time was a CDO. At this stage, it is sufficient to note that, in reaching that conclusion, his Honour had regard to the following matters.
	His Honour was not satisfied on the balance of probabilities that the respondent would substantially comply with the standard conditions of a supervision order. It followed that his Honour was not satisfied that a supervision order would adequately protect the community against the unacceptable risk that the respondent would commit a serious offence of stalking or violence. His Honour had regard to the findings he had made in respect of the respondent's propensity to commit serious offences and his pattern of offending, and to matters personal to the respondent, including: his personality traits; his refusal to take responsibility for his actions; his lack of insight into his offending and his severe alcohol abuse, which was a significant contributing factor to his offending; his failure to engage meaningfully with psychological treatment and programmes addressing his criminogenic needs, or to make any real gains from such interventions; his past 'complete disregard' for complying with court imposed orders; and the fact that he had minimal supports in the community. His Honour also concluded, having regard to the expert evidence and his own assessment, that the two properties that had been proposed for accommodation within the community were not suitable for the purposes of managing the respondent's risk under a supervision order.
	In making the CDO, Derrick J made several recommendations for measures that needed to be implemented that could affect the respondent's prospects of being released subject to a supervision at the first review of his detention.
	Section 64 of the HRSO Act requires that a person's detention under a CDO is to be reviewed periodically, and the first such review must be 'carried out … as soon as practicable after the end of [one] year commencing when the offender is first in custody on a day on which the offender would not have been in custody had the [CDO] not been made'. In the respondent's case, that date was 15 March 2024, so the first review was to be carried out as soon as practicable after 14 March 2025.
	The purpose of a review is to determine first whether the offender continues to be a high risk serious offender. If the court does not find that the offender remains a high risk serious offender, it must rescind the CDO. If the court finds that the offender remains a high risk serious offender, it must either affirm the CDO or rescind the CDO and make a supervision order, which is an order that the offender, when not in custody, is to be subject to stated conditions that the court considers appropriate, in accordance with s 30 of the HRSO Act. In essence, where the respondent remains a high risk serious offender, the court is required to determine whether the respondent's circumstances have changed, so that the protection of the community can be adequately achieved by a supervision order, instead of a CDO.
	On 8 October 2024, the applicant applied under s 64 of the HRSO Act for the first review of the respondent's detention to be listed as soon as practicable after 15 March 2025. On 29 November 2024, at a directions hearing held in accordance with s 66 of the HRSO Act, Whitby J made orders for the application to be heard on 26 March 2025 and for the respondent to be examined by a qualified expert, namely Professor Pyszora, for the purpose of preparing a report as required by s 74 of the HRSO Act, to be used on the hearing of the application, and other orders programming the application to a hearing, including an order that envisaged the preparation of a Treatment Progress Report and/or a Performance Report in respect of the respondent, although each of the reports subsequently prepared in the latter category was titled a Community Supervision Assessment (CSA).
	A report was subsequently prepared by Professor Pyszora on 10 February 2025. A Treatment Progress Report was prepared on 17 February 2025, and a CSA report was prepared on 18 March 2025. It is apt to note at this stage that the findings made by Professor Pyszora on her initial assessment of the respondent for these proceedings indicated that he continued to present an unacceptable risk of committing a serious offence in the nature of stalking or a violent offence, and that it remained necessary for him to be subject to a restriction order in order to ensure the adequate protection of the community against that risk. The question of whether a supervision order may provide adequate protection of the community was dependent the availability of suitable accommodation, the implementation of stringent conditions constraining his behaviours and associations, and adequate management of his health issues.
	Prior to the listed hearing date of 26 March 2025, the parties conferred and agreed that the listed hearing date of 26 March 2025 should be vacated, and the application adjourned, because the respondent at that stage did not have suitable accommodation available to him in the event that the court determined that it was otherwise appropriate to make a supervision order. As I will discuss later in these reasons, the availability of suitable accommodation is essential to the appropriateness of making a supervision order as the appropriate restriction order. The respondent was 67 years of age at that stage and, as will be explained below, he had, and continues to have, a number of health issues. It was considered that the prospect of the respondent finding suitable accommodation would depend on an Aged Care Assessment by the Western Australian Government's Aged Care Assessment Team (ACAT) being completed in respect of the respondent with a view to the respondent receiving Australian Government funded aged care services in the community. I will refer to the assessment as an 'ACAT assessment'. The hearing date was vacated accordingly, by consent of the parties, and the application was adjourned administratively to a further directions hearing to allow for Professor Pyszora's report of 10 February 2025 to be provided to the Department of Health for Western Australia for the purpose of conducting an ACAT assessment, and for the assessment of any other suitable services that might be available to the respondent. The court also ordered that an updated CSA concerning the outcome of the respondent's ACAT assessment be provided to the applicant at least seven days prior to the directions hearing.
	The matter then came before me for directions on 5 August 2025. Regrettably, meetings with State and Commonwealth Departments of Health to organise an ACAT assessment had not yet occurred. Although this meant that the matter was not ready for hearing at that time, the parties submitted that the application should be listed for hearing at a suitable date in the near future to provide a timeframe within which the ACAT assessment needed to occur. I ordered that the hearing be listed on a date to be fixed, no later than 19 December 2025, and made further programming orders. However, on 4 December 2025, by consent of the parties, further orders were made administratively, by which the application was listed to be heard on 6 February 2026. The ACAT assessment had been conducted in September 2025, but a support package had not yet been approved.
	The review hearing was conducted before me on 6 February 2026. By that stage, a further report, dated 27 January 2026, had been prepared by Professor Pyszora. In addition, there was a further Treatment Progress Report, dated 19 January 2026, and four further CSA reports, dated 29 May 2025, 30 July 2025, 19 November 2025 and 23 January 2026.
	At the commencement of the hearing, counsel for the applicant informed the court that the respondent was on a wait list as a 'medium priority participant' for a Support at Home Program package (SHP package). The estimated time for when the respondent might receive the package was July of 2026. Counsel submitted that, upon the respondent receiving such a package, it would be more likely that suitable accommodation would be found for the respondent in the community. The parties agreed that I should receive the evidence then available in the proceedings, including hearing from Professor Pyszora and two other witnesses, and that the hearing should then be adjourned partheard in the hope that the respondent would receive the SHP package in July 2026, and suitable accommodation could be confirmed for the respondent before I would be required to determine the application, in particular the question of whether it is appropriate to release the respondent on a supervision order. The matter proceeded accordingly.
	At the conclusion of the hearing, I adjourned the matter part heard to 20 July 2026. I ordered that a further CSA report be prepared by the Department of Justice, which was to include a desktop spatial analysis in respect of a proposed residential address. I also ordered that a supplementary report be prepared by Professor Pyszora, which was to include her opinion on the suitability of any proposed accommodation options and community management plans for the respondent as set out in the further CSA. Those reports were filed with the court on 1 July 2026 and 5 July 2026 respectively.
	On 20 July 2026, I received the further reports and heard oral evidence from Ms Kaitlyn Hoy, an Acting Senior Community Corrections Officer who is the respondent's case manager, and Professor Pyszora. The evidence at that stage was that aged care funding had been approved and was available to the respondent, a home care provider had been engaged, pursuant to a service agreement, accommodation was available to the respondent at a lodge, and he had paid a bond and rent for two weeks. Professor Pyszora considered the accommodation to be suitable for the management of the respondent's risk under a supervision order. The applicant submitted that, in light of the developments that had occurred, which would enhance the management of the respondent's risk in the community, the court could be satisfied that it is now appropriate to make a supervision order in respect of the respondent. Counsel for the respondent acknowledged that the evidence establishes that the respondent remains a high risk serious offender and submitted that the community could be adequately protected by the making of a supervision order.

	For the reasons that follow, I am satisfied that the respondent remains a high risk serious offender, but I am satisfied that there has been a change in his circumstances and that it is now appropriate to make a supervision order, enabling his risk to be managed in the community subject to conditions.

Statutory framework and legal principles
	At [6] and [8] above, I summarised the effect of pt 5 of the HRSO Act, under which the review is to be conducted. It is sufficient to add that s 66 of the HRSO Act provides for a directions hearing, which in this case occurred as described at [9] above.
	Section 67 of the HRSO Act provides that, unless the court otherwise orders, the chief executive officer (CEO) of the relevant Department (being the Department of Justice) must engage one or more qualified experts to prepare reports in accordance with s 74 to be used on the review. As I described above, an order was made by Whitby J in this case appointing Professor Pyszora as the qualified expert. Professor Pyszora was engaged on behalf of the CEO to prepare a report under s 74, and indeed prepared two additional reports because of the course the application took.
	Section 74 of the HRSO Act provides that a qualified expert who has been engaged to provide a report must examine the subject and prepare an independent report, which must indicate:
(1)	the reporter's assessment of the level of the risk that, without a restriction order, the subject will commit a serious offence; and
(2)	the reasons for the reporter's assessment.
	While I referred to the effect of s 68 of the HRSO Act at [8] above, it is appropriate to set out the provisions in full:
Review of detention under continuing detention order
(1)	On a review under section 66 of an offender's detention –
(a)	if the court does not find that the offender remains a high risk serious offender it must rescind the continuing detention order; or
(b)	if the court finds that the offender remains a high risk serious offender it must –
(i)	affirm the continuing detention order; or
(ii)	subject to section 29, rescind the continuing detention order and make a supervision order.
(2)	In deciding whether to make an order under subsection (1)(b)(i) or (ii), the paramount consideration is to be the need to ensure the adequate protection of the community.
	The structure of the provision requires the court to determine first whether the respondent remains a high risk serious offender.
	The definition of the term 'high risk serious offender' is contained in s 7(1) of the HRSO Act, which provides:
An offender is a high risk serious offender if the court dealing with an application under this Act finds that it is satisfied, by acceptable and cogent evidence and to a high degree of probability, that it is necessary to make a restriction order in relation to the offender to ensure adequate protection of the community against an unacceptable risk that the offender will commit a serious offence.
	Section 7(2) provides that the State has the onus of satisfying the court as required by subsection (1).
	Section 7(3) sets out a number of matters that the court must have regard to in determining whether a person is a high risk serious offender. They were set out by Derrick J in Hill [No 2] at [29]. They include any report prepared under s 74 of the HRSO Act.
	'Serious offence' is defined in s 5 of the HRSO Act. It is sufficient to note, as Derrick J did in Hill [No 2], that the respondent has been convicted of three offences that are serious offences within the meaning of the HRSO Act, being one offence of stalking contrary to s 338E(1)(b) of the Code, one offence of aggravated stalking contrary to s 338E(1)(a) of the Code and the offence of manslaughter contrary to s 280 of the Code. As noted at [5] above, in Hill [No 2], Derrick J found that the respondent poses a risk of committing offences of each of those types in the future, as well as, potentially, offences of unlawfully doing grievous bodily harm with intent to do grievous bodily harm or maim, disfigure or disable contrary to s 294 of the Code, unlawfully doing grievous bodily harm contrary to s 297 of the Code, and unlawful assault causing death contrary to s 281 of the Code, all of which are prescribed to be serious offences under the HRSO Act.
	It is apt to note that the provisions of s 68 are identical to the provisions of s 33 of the repealed Dangerous Sexual Offenders Act 2006 (WA) (DSO Act), save that it refers to 'high risk serious offender' rather than 'serious danger to the community'. The concepts and criteria with which the court is concerned in determining an application under s 64 of the HRSO Act are substantially the same as they were under the DSO Act, and, therefore, the jurisprudence established in respect of the DSO Act remains relevant in construing and applying the HRSO Act, with appropriate adaptation in cases involving nonsexual offences. While there is arguably a difference in the way the criteria in s 7(1) of the HRSO Act are to be applied, when compared with the criteria for finding a person to be a serious danger to the community under s 7 of the DSO Act, that difference does not affect the determination of this application. It is sufficient to note that s 7 of the HRSO Act requires first a determination of whether there is an unacceptable risk that the respondent will commit a serious offence, and secondly whether it is necessary to make a restriction order to ensure adequate protection of the community against that risk. As was conceded by the respondent, both criteria are met in this case.
	I note that nothing in the High Court's decision in Garlett v The State of Western Australia, which considered the constitutional validity of aspects of the HRSO Act, is inconsistent with adopting the jurisprudence established in respect of the DSO Act when construing and applying the HRSO Act. In fact, it is implicit from the majority judgments that such jurisprudence remains relevant.
	As to the principles relevant to determining whether a respondent is a high risk serious offender, I otherwise adopt the exposition by Derrick J in Hill [No 2] at [22]  [30].
	In Director of Public Prosecutions (WA) v Pindan [No 3], I considered the issue of whether, on a review of continuing detention under the DSO Act, the court was bound by findings in previous proceedings that had resulted in the CDO or the affirmation of the CDO, and the general approach to be taken on a review. In my opinion, the conclusions I reached in that case are relevant to review proceedings under the HRSO Act. Adapting those conclusions to the context of the HRSO Act, they may be summarised as follows:
(1)	On a review under s 68 of the HRSO Act, the court is required to decide, in accordance with s 7 of the HRSO Act, whether it is satisfied to a high degree of probability that the respondent remains a high risk serious offender. This requires a consideration of all the matters in s 7(3) on the evidence available at the time of the review.
(2)	If the court is not satisfied that the respondent remains a high risk serious offender, it must rescind the CDO.
(3)	If the court is satisfied that the respondent remains a high risk serious offender, it must determine whether the community could be adequately protected by the release of the respondent on a supervision order, being an order that the offender, when not in custody, is to be subject to stated conditions that the court considers appropriate, in accordance with s 30 of the HRSO Act. If the court is left in doubt that the community could be adequately protected in that way, it must affirm the CDO.
(4)	While the previous decision that the respondent was a high risk serious offender must be assumed to be correct and provides a point of departure for consideration of the first issue, it does not follow that the court at the next review must find that the respondent is a high risk serious offender if it finds there has been no change in his circumstances. The court is still required to be satisfied in accordance with s 7(1) of the HRSO Act that the respondent is a high risk serious offender at the time of the review. The onus remains on the State under s 7(2) to satisfy the court accordingly.
(5)	The task of determining the appropriate order for the adequate protection of the community is not constrained by the findings made in previous proceedings in respect of that issue, but those findings will be relevant as part of the background against which the current assessment is to be made.
(6)	The materials to which the court will have regard, and the previous findings on which it will rely, will depend on the manner in which the proceedings are conducted by the parties. Previous findings will usually provide the context for consideration of more recent developments in the respondent's circumstances. To use the terminology adopted in previous cases, they will provide the point of departure or baseline for the assessment of such developments.
(7)	The doctrine of issue estoppel may apply to some findings of fact made in previous proceedings, so that the parties will be bound by those findings.
(8)	If there is no challenge to a previous finding that is relevant to the determination of the issues on the annual review, the court should proceed on the basis that the finding was correct and give it weight in the current proceedings accordingly, unless there is evidence that, in the court's assessment, requires reconsideration of the finding. Such evidence may be in respect of facts that existed at the time of a previous decision, but which were not part of the evidence available at that time and have become known since then.
	Respectfully, the general approach was aptly summarised by Forrester J in The State of Western Australia v S [No 3] as follows:
It is a matter for the court conducting the review to independently decide whether the respondent is a high risk serious offender, and whether the CDO should be affirmed, although the court conducting the review is entitled to have regard to, and give weight to, views expressed in earlier reviews and the original application.
	I also note that a finding under s 68(1)(b) that an offender 'remains a high risk serious offender' is a finding that it remains necessary to make a restriction order in relation to the offender to ensure adequate protection of the community against an unacceptable risk that the offender will commit a serious offence, which necessarily entails a finding that if the offender is not subject to a restriction order the community will not be adequately protected against an unacceptable risk that the offender will commit a serious offence.
	If the court finds the respondent remains a high risk serious offender, then, in deciding whether to affirm the CDO or release the respondent on a supervision order, the court should choose the order that is least invasive of the person's right to be at liberty, whilst ensuring an adequate degree of protection of the community, which is the paramount consideration under s 68(2) of the HRSO Act. Continuing detention should not be ordered unless that course is justified by the circumstances existing at the time of the review.
	However, that question is affected by the fact that the discretion under s 68(1)(b)(ii) (set out above) to make a supervision order is subject to s 29 of the HRSO Act, which provides:
Limitation on power to make or amend supervision order
(1)	A court cannot make, affirm or amend a supervision order in relation to an offender unless it is satisfied, on the balance of probabilities, that the offender will substantially comply with the standard conditions of the order as made, affirmed or amended.
(2)	The onus of proof as to the matter described in subsection (1) is on the offender.
(3)	This section does not apply to the making of an interim supervision order.
	The term 'standard conditions' in relation to a supervision order is defined in s 3 of the HRSO Act to mean a condition that, under s 30(2) of the HRSO Act, must be included in the order. Section 30(2) of the HRSO Act specifies seven conditions that must be included in any supervision order. Those conditions are set out in the supervision order annexed to these reasons (Annexure). They include a condition that the respondent will not commit a serious offence during the period of the supervision order.
	The effect of s 29(1) and s 29(2) of the HRSO Act is that, before the court can make a supervision order in relation to a respondent, he must satisfy the court, on the balance of probabilities, that he will substantially comply with the standard conditions set out in s 30(2).
	The words 'substantially comply with' should be given their ordinary meaning, consistent with the purposes of the legislation and of the general conditions of a supervision order. In essence, I must be satisfied that the respondent will comply with the standard conditions in a manner and to an extent that is consistent with, and will enable the attainment of, the general object of the supervision order and the legislation, namely the adequate protection of the community by management and mitigation of the risk that the respondent will commit a serious offence.
	As I identified in Hart, some matters that will be of relevance are (a) the respondent's attitude to the conditions of the supervision order; (b) his capacity to comply with the conditions; and (c) what measures there are in place to ensure he would substantially comply.
	In determining whether to affirm the CDO or make a supervision order, the following principles established by authorities under both the repealed DSO Act and the HRSO Act are relevant.
	Detention under the HRSO Act is not punishment for past offending; it is a protective mechanism designed to prevent the risk of future serious offending (of a relevant kind) from being realised. As Hall J noted in Director of Public Prosecutions (WA) v Unwin [No 3], it is a significant thing to deprive a person of his liberty, not for something he has done but for something that he might do in the future. Further, the purposes of continuing detention include treatment and care of the offender in a secure environment. The objective of treatment is to reduce risk. If the offender's circumstances change, or if there is a change in external circumstances, such that the offender's risk of reoffending reduces, it may be that the adequate protection of the community against the risk, although it is still unacceptable in the absence of any restrictive measures, can be achieved by the making of a supervision order. As Beech J (as his Honour then was) said in Director of Public Prosecutions (WA) v DAL [No 2]:
The [DSO Act] does not require that there be no risk of reoffending. Such a requirement could never be met and would mean no person to whom the [DSO Act] applies would ever be released. The question is whether the risk is reduced to a reasonably acceptable level that ensures adequate protection of the community. That requires a weighing of the nature and degree of risk in the context of methods for the management and reduction of that risk.
	Accordingly, the level of risk posed by the respondent, and whether the community can be protected adequately against the risk under a supervision order, must be assessed by having regard not only to factors personal to the respondent, such as personality, attitudes, propensities and his capacity to control or manage his own behaviour, but also to external measures and obligations that can be put in place under a supervision order to mitigate the risk. The external measures include the available means to monitor, supervise and treat the respondent.
	All of the factors to which I have referred affect both the question of whether the respondent will substantially comply with the standard conditions of a supervision order and the overall efficacy of the supervision order in ensuring adequate protection of the community. Gains made by the respondent in treatment and his behaviour while in custody will inform the assessment of the personal factors. The availability of new technology or resources in the community will be relevant to the assessment of external factors.
	The review process is intended to ensure that detention only continues where necessary. However, as noted above, if after considering all the evidence, the court is left in doubt as to whether the conditions of a supervision order would adequately protect the community, then, because the paramount consideration is the need to ensure the adequate protection of the community, the court must affirm the CDO. Further, in assessing the likely efficacy of a supervision order to sufficiently reduce the respondent's risk of serious offending and provide adequate protection of the community against that risk, the availability of suitable accommodation is a critical consideration. It is not simply a place for the person who is subject to a supervision order to live. The location and type of accommodation are factors that are integral to any proper assessment of the risk of reoffending.

However, the various considerations under s 7(3) of the HRSO Act overlap in many respects. For instance, the matters in paragraphs (h) and (i), concerning the respondent's risk of committing a serious offence if not subject to a restriction order, and the need to protect the community, involve conclusions that directly inform the question of whether the respondent is a high risk serious offender. Consideration of the questions concerning propensity and any pattern of offending behaviour (paragraphs (c) and (d)) will be informed by the respondent's antecedents and criminal record (paragraph (g)) and the findings in the reports and assessments referred to in paragraphs (a) and (b). Analysis and findings
	Having regard to all of the evidence in these proceedings and the findings made in Hill [No 2], I have reached the following conclusions in respect of the issues I must determine.
The respondent remains a high risk serious offender
	The question of whether the respondent remains a high risk serious offender requires consideration of the findings made in Hill [No 2] and the evidence in respect of his circumstances since then that bears on the factors that inform that question under s 7(3) of the HRSO Act.
	I am satisfied by a combination of factors that the respondent has a propensity to commit serious offences, as identified by Derrick J in Hill [No 2], being:
(1)	serious offences comprised of stalking offences against family members and against females with whom he has been in a social or intimate relationship, or with whom he is in a social or intimate relationship; and
(2) 	serious offences comprised of violent offences against females with whom he has been in a social or intimate relationship, or with whom he is in a social or intimate relationship.
	The factors that, in combination, establish that propensity are:
(1)	the respondent's history of offending;
(2)	the attitudes he has expressed over the years to various professionals, including in the past three years;
(3)	his lack of insight into his offending and the complex factors contributing to it;
(4)	his belief that he has done nothing wrong in respect of most of his offending conduct and inappropriate behaviours;
(5)	the problematic behaviours he has exhibited on occasions in custody and at medical appointments, including for a period since the CDO was made (as set out in detail in my discussion of the evidence);
(6)	his psychopathic personality traits; and
(7)	his tendency to engage in predatory behaviour towards women, especially older women, from whom he believes he can gain a benefit.
	I also agree with the conclusion reached by Derrick J that there have been patterns to much of the respondent's offending, including the routine contravention of VROs that have been taken out against him by members of his family, the commission of stalking offences against vulnerable females with whom he has developed an intimate or social relationship, and the commission of serious acts of violence against women, particularly older women, with whom he is, or has been, in an intimate relationship. Although alcohol intoxication has not necessarily been implicated in all of the respondent's offending, a pattern in some of his offending has been that he has been intoxicated with alcohol. Another pattern I consider to be present in his offending is the escalation of grievances from transactional matters, or from rejection, into threatening, intimidatory or violent behaviour, in the context of a grandiose personality and a tendency to anger, particularly when affected by alcohol.
	I am satisfied on the basis of Professor Pyszora's detailed assessment of the respondent, which I have set out in detail and accept as cogent and persuasive, that the respondent presents a high risk of committing a serious offence of intimate partner violence or stalking as described by Professor Pyszora in her evidence concerning risk scenarios, set out at [242] to [245] above. That assessment of risk is informed by a multitude of historical and dynamic factors, the significance of which was explained by Professor Pyszora. Apart from the broader scenarios, especially of serious violent offending, there is a very obvious way in which the respondent is at high risk of committing a serious offence. As was found by Derrik J in Hill [No 2], the respondent is clearly at a high risk of contravening the two VROs that are currently in force against him.
	The impact on victims if the respondent was to commit a serious offence could be serious physical trauma, including death in the worst case scenario, and/or serious and potentially enduring psychological trauma. That has been the outcome for at least some the respondent's victims of his serious offending. Stalking is a serious offence for the very reason that it can result in significant psychological trauma to the victim. There is a paramount need to protect the community, especially vulnerable persons with whom the respondent may come into contact, from the danger the respondent poses. Although the respondent suffers from physical ailments that might reduce his capacity to inflict physical violence, it is by no means clear that he lacks such a capacity, especially against vulnerable older women. It is noteworthy that the respondent, at a relatively recent point in time, made a threat to a prison officer that he would 'see [him] on the outside', which I am satisfied was a threat of future violence and suggests the respondent does not regard himself as being incapable of violence. In any event, the respondent's capacity for stalking by electronic or other means, which would not require physical contact, is unimpeded.
	I am satisfied that the respondent has considerable outstanding treatments needs. His continuing failure to accept responsibility for most of his prior serious offending, in particular the manslaughter offence, remains a significant concern. His lack of insight into his wrongdoing, combined with his psychopathic traits, means that he does not have the internal resources to mitigate his risk of committing a serious offence if circumstances arose that fall into the risk scenarios described by Professor Pyszora. While he has some understanding of the contribution of alcohol intoxication to his prior offending, and he appreciates the need to avoid alcohol, his risk management plans are superficial, essentially amounting to an overinflated estimation of his capacity to choose to avoid alcohol. Given that his risk of committing a serious offence would be elevated in the event he becomes intoxicated, his failure to properly address this risk factor underscores the assessment that he presents a high risk of committing a serious offence.
	In summary, I am not satisfied that the respondent could manage his own risk of committing a serious offence in the community. Nor, in my view, are there measures short of the stringent monitoring and supervision that would be implemented pursuant to the conditions of a supervision order, as recommended by Professor Pyszora, that could sufficiently mitigate the respondent's risk and provide adequate protection of the community. Without a restriction order, the respondent's risk would be unacceptable, and the community would not be adequately protected against that risk.
	Consequently, I am satisfied to a high degree of probability that there continues to be an unacceptable risk that the respondent will commit a serious offence of violence, in particular intimate partner violence, and/or stalking, if he is not subject to some form of restriction, and that it is necessary for the respondent to be subject to a restriction order to ensure adequate protection of the community against the unacceptable risk. Anything short of a restriction order would not provide adequate protection of the community. I am satisfied that the respondent requires a high level of supervision and monitoring and ongoing counselling, at least in relation to behaviour management (even if he does not gain proper insight into his offending), if his risk is to be adequately managed.
	Therefore, I am satisfied that the respondent remains a high risk serious offender. The respondent's concession in that regard was properly made.
Developments in the respondent's circumstances
	Based on the evidence before Derrick J, and the respondent's circumstances as they were at that time, I respectfully consider that his Honour's conclusion that a CDO was the appropriate restriction order at that time was justified. The question now is whether there has been a change in the respondent's circumstances that supports the conclusion that a CDO is no longer necessary for the purposes of providing adequate protection of the community because such protection can be achieved by the conditions of a supervision order. Before I can reach that conclusion, I must be satisfied that the respondent will substantially comply with the standard conditions of a supervision order, which, under s 30 of the HRSO Act, include not committing a serious offence and complying with all reasonable directions of his supervising CCO.
	Having regard to the following matters, I am satisfied that the respondent's circumstances have changed in a material way in several respects.
	The first matter is that the respondent has engaged in individual counselling in a meaningful manner. Although it is questionable whether he has made any gains at this stage, and his reporting of what has been discussed in counselling suggests an ongoing lack of insight into the purpose of psychological intervention, he has indicated that he is prepared to continue to engage in counselling. At this stage it appears that counselling will continue to be provided by Mr Summerton, with whom the respondent appears to have developed a good rapport. It would be hoped that over the longer term, Mr Summerton will be able to address criminogenic factors with the respondent in a manner that will lead to the respondent achieving some insight into his offending and the factors contributing to his offending, and developing attitudes, skills and strategies that will enable him to manage his risk without external controls. In the meantime, there is scope for Mr Summerton to be part of the process of monitoring the respondent's attitudes and behaviours and assisting him to deal with stressors in the community, to reduce the risk of the respondent relapsing into alcohol use and other risk-laden behaviours. There will also be an opportunity for Mr Summerton, who is engaging in a therapeutic rather than supervisory role, to redirect the respondent's behaviours if it appears he is drifting into problematic relationships or behaviours. In summary, counselling has the capacity to contribute to the management and mitigation of the respondent's risk. The respondent's preparedness to engage in counselling is a change from the reluctance noted by Derrick J in Hill [No 2].
	Secondly, in the last 12 months, the respondent has not been recorded as engaging in negative incidents in prison of a kind involving inappropriate behaviour relevant to his risk of committing a serious offence. Behaviour that has been relevant in that regard previously has included sexualised comments to female staff and patients, when the respondent has been at hospital, and aggressive or threatening behaviour. In more recent times it appears that the respondent's behaviour has improved. Although he does not think that he has consciously done anything to improve his behaviour, because he does not believe that he had been doing anything wrong previously, Professor Pyszora was of the view, nevertheless, that the apparent improvement has some significance in assessing whether his risk can be managed pursuant to a supervision order. I accept that to be the case.
	Thirdly, the respondent has shown some change in his attitude towards supervision and the availability to him of practical support in the community. He appears now to be willing to accept the home care (and transport) support that will be provided to him in the community, whereas previously he had been somewhat resistant to the provision of such support or to accepting that he will require such support in the community. There is no doubt that he will require support. In my view, the support that his care workers will provide to him, according to the items specified by Ms Hoy from the care package, will assist him in his day-to-day living, and, in doing so, can contribute to the reduction of stress in his life that might otherwise cause him to decompensate in a way that may elevate his risk of committing a serious offence. Furthermore, the presence of care or support workers, at least while they are with him, will potentially contribute to the mitigation of the risk of the respondent engaging in behaviour that may be a precursor to serious offending. Provided Professor Pyszora's recommendation concerning robust exchange of information is implemented, the care workers may also provide some assistance in the monitoring of the respondent's behaviour. That may include indications that he is not coping, which can then be addressed in supervision or counselling sessions. As Ms Hoy said, the support workers will be able to contact a CCO at the HRSO Unit directly if a relevant behavioural issue arises.
	Such support as I have described was not available at the time of Derrick J's decision. In fact, it was a significant factor militating against the making of a supervision order (including the question of whether the respondent would substantially comply) that he did not have any significant community supports.
	Fourthly, the accommodation that is now available to the respondent is a significant change of circumstance. Although it is being provided by the same Lodge provider who had offered accommodation to the respondent at the Lodge property considered by Derrick J, the second Lodge property is a different property. It does not have some of the problems identified in respect of the first Lodge property, such as the fact that the Lodge property was in relatively close proximity to where previous victims were living, and the fact that there was a potential for women to visit the premises. The second Lodge property also appears to be a different kind of property, with a significantly larger number of rooms, one of which will be assigned to the respondent. All the occupants are men, and it appears there is a prohibition on women visiting the premises. It also appears that the respondent may be able to occupy a room at ground level, which will be to his benefit, given that he has mobility problems.
	It is significant that, having regard to the differences presented by the second Lodge property, Professor Pyszora considers it to be suitable accommodation. Ms Hoy, and before her, Ms Jones, indicated that the HRSO Unit and WA Police will be able to manage the respondent's risk at that accommodation. No material issue was identified in the DSA. The Lodge proprietor and manager is familiar with and appears to have a good relationship with the HRSO Unit and is familiar with the sorts of issues that may arise with persons who are subject to the HRSO Act. It can be expected that she will cooperate with the HRSO Unit in reporting any inappropriate behaviour by the respondent, in particular towards her or the other female staff member.
	The respondent can be monitored by GPS monitoring, and it will be possible for his CCO to stipulate exclusion zones to prevent him going to places where his risk might be elevated.
	I am satisfied, having regard to the evidence of Professor Pyszora and the evidence of Ms Hoy and Ms Jones, that the room offered to the respondent at the second Lodge property is suitable accommodation, and that the respondent's risk can be managed at those premises, provided he conducts himself in a way that does not lead to an escalation of his risk, and that he substantially complies with the standard conditions of the supervision order.
	Finally, the respondent appears to have demonstrated a change of attitude to the efforts that have been made by the HRSO Unit in ensuring that the respondent received an aged care funding package and suitable support through Encompass Home Care. His gratitude suggests the potential for him to appreciate the support he will receive through supervision in the community, and to be prepared to comply with reasonable directions that are intended to manage his risk, and, in doing so, to assist him avoid returning to prison. As he did at the time of the restriction order proceedings, the respondent has expressed motivation and willingness to comply with the conditions of a supervision order. I am satisfied that those expressions of motivation and willingness may now be given more weight.
The appropriateness of a supervision order
	Having regard to the changes in the respondent's circumstances, and applying the principles I discussed in Hart, I am satisfied on the balance of probabilities that he will substantially comply with the standard conditions of the supervision order. In reaching that conclusion, while I have had regard to all of the matters I have referred to in the preceding section, I have given particular weight to the impression formed by Professor Pyszora and by Ms Hoy that the respondent appears to be genuinely motivated to remain out of prison and to comply with the conditions of the proposed supervision order, of which he has been made aware. He understands that contravening the conditions of the supervision order may result in him being arrested and returned to prison, with the potential for indefinite detention pursuant to a CDO. I am satisfied that the respondent's compliance will be assisted by the support he will receive from the care workers pursuant to the aged care package, and by the counselling he will receive from Mr Summerton. Further, as the authorities recognise, while internal motivation and resources will always be important considerations in determining whether a respondent will substantially comply with the standard conditions of a supervision order, such compliance, particularly with the condition that he must not commit a serious offence, can be achieved by effective external constraints, supervision and monitoring. The raft of conditions in the supervision order will impose such constraints and allow for such supervision and monitoring.  He will have obligations of disclosure that will assist in the monitoring of his risk. In particular, the conditions will allow for monitoring of whether the respondent has relapsed into the use of alcohol, which is a major risk factor and would be a marker of imminent risk of serious offending.
	The matters that inform the issue of substantial compliance also inform the question of whether the conditions of the proposed supervision order will be effective to ensure adequate protection of the community, by reducing the respondent's risk of committing a serious offence to an acceptable level. A significant consideration in respect of the latter issue is that, in Professor Pyszora's opinion, if the respondent is subject to stringent supervision and control under a supervision order, his risk of committing a serious offence would not be imminent. That is because the HRSO management team should be able to identify at a sufficiently early stage any indicators of a high-risk situation and can take action to prevent the risk from being realised. Such behaviour would ordinarily constitute or be related to a contravention of a condition of the supervision order. Depending on the nature of the behaviour, measures short of contravention might be appropriate, such as warnings, directions, counselling or prosecution under s 80 of the HRSO Act, without contravention proceedings. However, if the indicators are 'red flags' that the risk of serious offending has become more imminent, it would be expected that the respondent would be subject to contravention proceedings before this court, so that the level of his risk and the efficacy of the supervision order can be reassessed, if he is found to have contravened a requirement of the order.
	In short, the risk of the respondent committing a serious offence is not currently imminent if he were to be subject to stringent supervision and control, and the supervision order would enable identification of an increase in imminence and the taking of action that would ensure the adequate protection of the community.
	I am satisfied that the conditions that have been proposed, which were discussed at length in the proceedings and have been amended after such discussion, will provide adequate protection of the community. The conditions are set out in the Annexure. While many of the conditions are commonly found in supervision orders made in respect of high risk serious offenders, that is because they are necessary and appropriate in most cases to address common risk factors. The conditions in this case have been formulated to address the respondent's risk factors as described above, and, more specifically, they reflect Professor Pyszora's recommendations, which I have set out in detail. The purpose of many of the conditions will be self-evident, having regard to those recommendations. I will address the purpose of some of the conditions below.
	The condition in respect of employment and education (condition 10), which prohibits the respondent from starting or changing employment, education, training or volunteer work is necessary because all such activities would involve interactions with other people and the opportunity for the respondent to come into contact with and develop relationships with vulnerable women, or to become involved in transactional relationships that could lead to grievances and an increased risk of serious offending. It will be necessary for the agencies monitoring and managing the respondent's risk to be able to assess the suitability of particular employment, volunteer work or educational activities for the respondent, having regard to his risk factors and any measures that may be necessary to mitigate risk if he engages in the proposed employment volunteer work or educational activity.
	The conditions relating to treatment and mental health (conditions 11 to 13) are necessary and appropriate, having regard to my findings in respect of the respondent's outstanding treatment needs. The condition concerning anti-depressant treatment, which depends on the respondent's consent in any event, is appropriate given that depression has been an issue for him and is a risk factor for relapse into alcohol use. The need for disclosure of information is to enable appropriate monitoring of the respondent's risk and the formulation of appropriate measures to deal with increased risk.
	Apart from requiring the respondent to report to police, which is an appropriate measure to enable monitoring of his circumstances, the conditions allow police to search the respondent's person, his premises and any vehicle (condition 15). An obvious purpose for such a condition is to enable the identification of any articles that may indicate that the respondent has entered into a relationship of a kind that would present as a risk factor, as discussed above, or that might constitute weapons, given the violent risk scenarios identified by Professor Pyszora.
	The conditions relating to disclosure reflect Professor Pyszora's opinion, which I have accepted, that there must be robust exchange of information if the respondent's risk is to be properly monitored.
	The conditions in respect of previous victims (conditions 18 to 20) are adequate, in my view, to protect them against unwanted contact from the respondent. Unlike in the proceedings before Derrick J, there was no submission from any victim in the present proceedings, but I have had regard to the fact that any contact from the respondent, including inadvertent contact in public, could be traumatic for the victims. The HRSO Act is concerned with adequate protection of the community against the commission of a serious offence. The potential for inadvertent contact with a victim cannot be a barrier to a respondent's release if his risk of committing a serious offence can be adequately managed under a supervision order. The conditions would require the respondent to report any direct or indirect contact with victims, which will enable the HRSO Unit to assess whether any steps need to be taken to ensure the well-being of such victims.
	The conditions relating to criminal conduct prohibit the respondent from committing any offence that has a custodial penalty, not just serious offences. The condition recognises that the respondent's risk of committing a serious offence may increase if his behaviour deteriorates to the point of engaging in criminal conduct, even of a lesser kind, as it would indicate the respondent's antisocial traits manifesting. It is appropriate that any offending that falls within the condition be assessed for its significance to the respondent's risk and whether it is necessary to commence contravention proceedings if there is a reasonable belief that his risk of committing a serious offence has increased.
	Condition 23, which prohibits a breach of the Misuse of Drugs Act 1981 (WA) is concerned mainly with the respondent engaging in drug use. While his main substance risk factor is alcohol, if he were to resort to illicit drug use, he may well be more vulnerable to relapsing into drinking alcohol. In addition, some drugs, such as methylamphetamine, are known to be associated with aggressive behaviour. Given the respondent's risk profile, the use of such a drug would be of particular concern. I am satisfied the condition is appropriate.
	Similarly, while the respondent's serious violent offences have not involved the use of firearms or other weapons, I consider that condition 24 is appropriate, having regard to Professor Pyszora's opinion about the potential for escalation of violence in any future risk scenario. If the respondent were to be in possession of any weapon as described in the condition, it would be a matter of concern, given there would be no legitimate reason for him possessing such an item.
	Condition 25, which regulates the respondent's conduct towards any officer of the Department or of any other agency performing a function under the supervision order is appropriate, given some of the respondent's past behaviour towards prison officers and what might be regarded as insulting behaviour towards medical staff. Such behaviour is related to his risk factors.
	I am satisfied the curfew conditions are appropriate, having regard to Professor Pyszora's opinion that a curfew would provide the respondent with structure in his daily routine during his reintegration, which will reduce the prospect of stressful circumstances causing the respondent to decompensate. It is not intended as a long term risk mitigation measure, and it will be appropriate for the respondent's supervising CCO to assess, in consultation with Mr Summerton (or such other psychologist as may be treating the respondent), when the curfew condition may be relaxed or dispensed with, having regard to the respondent's circumstances.
	The conditions concerning checking of electronic devices (conditions 28 to 34) are appropriate, having regard to the opinion of Professor Pyszora, which I have accepted, that such conditions will be an important risk management tool to monitor any new associations the respondent may make, or any attempts by him to contact previous victims. Any attempts by the respondent to conceal his electronic activity would be an indicator of behaviour that suggests an elevated risk of serious offending.
	The need for the conditions concerning interactions with females and the formation of relationships (conditions 35 to 38) should be selfevident, having regard to Professor Pyszora's evidence about such matters, as discussed above. In her report of 1 May 2026, Ms Hoy noted that Professor Pyszora, in discussion with her about the supervision order, was of the view that 'because of [the respondent's] unreliability in truthfully disclosing his associations and communications, cross-checking his diary entries with his digital data (through device-checking) and interagency collaboration will be pivotal in detecting early warnings signs of risk.' I am satisfied, on that basis, that the diary condition (condition 44), is also appropriate.
	The conditions relating to alcohol use include a requirement that the respondent be subject to testing, which may include blood testing. Given that alcohol intoxication is a major risk factor for the respondent, and that it would significantly increase the imminence of him committing a serious offence, I am satisfied that the condition is appropriate. Breath testing may suffice most of the time, but blood testing may be necessary for confirmation of a significant reading. Urinalysis, which is less invasive, is not suitable for the respondent for reasons discussed above.
	I am satisfied that the combination of the conditions, which are sufficient, but no more than is necessary, to adequately manage the respondent's risk in the community, will ensure adequate protection of the community.
Duration of the supervision order
	As for the duration of the supervision order, I accept the evidence of Professor Pyszora that a period of five years is appropriate. I am satisfied, on the basis of Professor Pyszora's opinion, that a period less than five years would not be sufficient to enable the respondent to reach a point where he could self-manage his risk of committing a serious offence. Given his outstanding treatment needs, it is likely to take a very significant period before his risk can be adequately mitigated without the stringent restrictions and monitoring provided for by the supervision order. However, if the respondent demonstrates changes in his insights and further changes in his behaviour, such as to indicate lessening risk, it would be expected that the implementation of the supervision order would be relaxed over time, to the extent that the conditions allow, so as to give the respondent the opportunity to demonstrate his capacity for self-management. However, should it be apparent within the last year of the supervision order that the respondent's risk is still at a level where he could not be entrusted to self-manage his risk, the State can make an application for a further supervision order. I note that the respondent is now 68 years of age, so he will be in his early 70s at that time. It may be that his physical condition will have a bearing on his level of risk at that time, but that will be a matter to be determined having regard to all relevant circumstances.
Date of commencement of supervision order
	As to when the order should commence, I referred at [218] above to Ms Hoy's evidence that it could be given effect 21 days after the order is made.  I have inferred from her evidence that the HRSO Unit will require 21 days to ensure there is sufficient time to take all measures that are necessary for the effective implementation and administration of the supervision order. On that basis, I am not satisfied that the implementation of the order from an earlier date is practically feasible, and the commencement date will be 7 September 2026, being 21 days after the date on which the order will be made. The only concern was as to whether the proposed accommodation would still be available. The evidence was that the proprietor of the second Lodge property has generally been very flexible, and that, provided further payment of rent is made to hold the room, the room will be held for the respondent.
Conclusion
	It follows from my findings that the CDO must be rescinded and that I should make a supervision order. I will make an order in the terms of the Annexure.
Suppression order
	The applicant, at the request of the Department of Justice, sought an order that the address of the proposed accommodation and the name of the provider be the subject of a non-publication order and be anonymised or redacted in these reasons. The court is aware of past matters where the safety of persons who are the subject of supervision orders (the supervised person), and of Departmental staff who are responsible for administering supervision orders, has been compromised because members of the public have gone to the supervised person's accommodation and have engaged in intimidating conduct towards that person or Departmental staff. It is noteworthy that the property provided by the Lodge provider serves an important purpose in enabling persons who have been imprisoned to be accommodated when, otherwise, they may have difficulty finding accommodation.
	I am satisfied that the order sought is appropriate. Accordingly, I have used terminology for the proposed address and the provider that anonymises the address and the name of the provider, and I have redacted the address in the Annexure.
	As the address was referred to in the proceedings and in exhibits filed in the proceedings, I will also make a non-publication order.
Orders
	The CDO made by Derrick J on 15 March 2024 is rescinded.
	I order that, when not in custody, the respondent is to be subject to the conditions of the supervision order in the Annexure.
	I order that there is to be no publication of the address of the accommodation to which the respondent is to be released, or of the name of the provider of the accommodation, except to the extent that it is necessary for the parties or any government department or other agency to communicate the address to any person involved in the administration of the supervision order in respect of the respondent dated 17 August 2026, or is otherwise involved in the management of the respondent's risk in the community.
