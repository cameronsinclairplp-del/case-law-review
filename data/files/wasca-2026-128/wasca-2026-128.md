# Malone Darcy Fleming (a pseudonym) v The State of Western Australia [2026] WASCA 128

## One line
Court of Appeal upholds a 5 year 4 month total effective sentence for a meth dealer sentenced as 'just under' mid-level, confirming that reoffending while on bail justifies cumulative sentencing and does not breach the totality principle.

## What happened
FLEMING (a pseudonym) pleaded guilty to three counts. Count 1: possession of a trafficable quantity of methylamphetamine (176.38g, 78-81% purity) with intent to sell or supply, found in a Perth hotel room safe during a search warrant, contrary to Misuse of Drugs Act 1981 (WA) s 6(1)(a) read with s 34(1)(a). Count 2: possession of $17,200 reasonably suspected of being unlawfully obtained (found in a bedside drawer), contrary to Criminal Code (WA) s 417(1); the appellant admitted this was proceeds of meth sales. His phone, seized and interrogated in that search, contained messages about the sale and supply of prohibited drugs and paperwork consistent with a tick list. Count 3: possession of methylamphetamine (22.77g) with intent to sell or supply, contrary to Misuse of Drugs Act 1981 (WA) s 6(1)(a) — this offending occurred about six months later while he was on bail, when police searched his vehicle at a regional police station and found the drug plus digital scales, empty clip seal bags, glass smoking implements and two metal spoons. SHEPHERD DCJ characterised him as 'just under a mid-level dealer' and sentenced him to 4 years (count 1), 12 months concurrent (count 2) and 1 year 4 months cumulative (count 3, reduced from 2 years for totality) — a total effective sentence of 5 years 4 months. He appealed on a single ground: that the total effective sentence breached the first limb of the totality principle. The notice was lodged seven weeks late.

## What the Court held
The Court refused the extension of time, refused leave and dismissed the appeal. It held the total effective sentence of 5 years 4 months was not unreasonable or plainly unjust and did not infringe the first limb of the totality principle. The individual sentences were not challenged. The Court held it was plainly correct to order partial cumulation for count 3 because reoffending while on bail — with an offence of similar nature — is a significantly aggravating factor showing disregard for the law and enhancing the need for personal deterrence. The reduction of count 3 from 2 years to 1 year 4 months adequately reflected totality. The Court reiterated that for serious drug dealing the major considerations are general and personal deterrence, that quantity and purity matter as indicators of potential harm, and that personal circumstances carry less weight in this class of offence. It found the comparable cases advanced by both sides of limited utility, being either pre-dating the increase to a life maximum or involving couriers rather than dealers, but broadly consistent with the sentence imposed.

## What it means for your casework
This is a sentencing appeal, so it does not change how you investigate, but it reinforces points worth knowing. The investigative work here — search warrant on the hotel room with the seizure and interrogation of the phone (sale/supply messages, tick list), and six months later the vehicle search at a regional police station while he was on bail — built a clear commercial-dealing case, and the admission that the $17,200 was drug proceeds supported both the s 417(1) count and the finding of an ongoing enterprise. Note how strongly the courts treat reoffending while on bail: it is significantly aggravating and justifies cumulative punishment, so flag any suspect who offends while on conditional release. The case confirms that purity and quantity evidence, and material showing commerciality (tick lists, scales, packaging, cash in mixed denominations), materially strengthen the seriousness of a drug charge.

## Verdict
AWARENESS only — this is a sentencing/totality appeal that turns on its own facts and states no new point of principle; it does not alter investigative or charging practice.

Applicable legal principles on the appeal
35

The principles that apply in this State when sentencing an offender
in relation to serious drug dealing offences are so well established that
they need no recitation of authority. In summary:
1.

The major sentencing considerations for offences of dealing or
trafficking in dangerous drugs of addiction are general and
personal deterrence.

2.

The weight of the drugs in question is not, generally, the chief
factor to be taken into account in fixing a sentence. However, it

is a matter of importance. That is because it can be presumed
that the greater the quantity and the purity, the greater the harm
which may be done to the community.
3.

Other matters to be taken into account include: (a) the nature
and level of the offender's participation in drug dealing or
trafficking within a particular organisation or generally; and
(b) whether the offending was committed for commercial gain.
The degree of purity is also often regarded as significant.

4.

Matters personal to an offender will almost always be
subsidiary considerations, but they are not completely
irrelevant.

36

The nature and level of an offender's participation in a drug
enterprise may be a significant sentencing factor. However, the
characterisation of the offender's role, where possible, must not obscure
the sentencing court's evaluative assessment of the offender's criminal
conduct as charged and found. An offender is sentenced not for his or
her role or place in a hierarchy but for a particular dealing with a
particular drug at a particular time.

37

Assessment of the single ground of appeal
62

We are not satisfied that the total effective sentence of 5 years and
4 months' immediate imprisonment is unreasonable or plainly unjust
such that a substantial wrong has occurred or there has been some
misapplication of principle.

63

The appellant's offending was serious. There is, and could be, no
complaint about the sentence of 4 years' immediate imprisonment
imposed in respect of count 1. Having regard to the quantity and purity
of the methylamphetamine in the appellant's possession for sale or
supply, and that the appellant was carrying on a drug dealing enterprise
for commercial gain, the individual sentence imposed for count 1 was
relatively moderate even taking into account the considerable
mitigating factors available to the appellant. The additional criminality
inherent in the offending the subject of count 2 did not result in
additional imprisonment to be served by the appellant. It was,
however, open to the sentencing judge to provide for a degree of
cumulacy in respect of the possession of the $17,200 that was
reasonably suspected to have been unlawfully obtained. See VXM
[63] - [66], [68], [77]; Howarth v The State of Western Australia.30 In
providing that the sentence imposed on count 2 was to be served

The sentencing judge was plainly correct to provide for a degree
of cumulacy in relation to the sentence imposed on count 3. The
offending the subject of count 3 occurred some six months after the
offending the subject of counts 1 and 2. It occurred while the appellant
was on bail for the offending the subject of counts 1 and 2. The
commission of an offence while on some form of conditional release
from custody is an aggravating factor, particularly where the offence is
of a similar nature to the offending or alleged offending that brought
about the offender's conditional release. It demonstrates that the
offender has disregard for the law and is prepared to offend despite
having been granted liberty on condition of being on good behaviour.
It displays contempt for the law and enhances the need for personal
deterrence.

It was necessary, so as properly to mark the appellant's overall
criminality, to order the partial accumulation of the sentence for the
second offence of possession of methylamphetamine with intent to sell
or supply (ie count 3) with the sentence for the initial offence of
possession of methylamphetamine with intent to sell or supply
(ie count 1).

66

The sentencing judge reduced the individual sentence for count 3
from 2 years' immediate imprisonment to 1 year and 4 months'
immediate imprisonment in the application of the totality principle.
That cumulative component reflected offending that was temporally
distinct, involved a further quantity of high purity methylamphetamine
intended for sale or supply and was committed while the appellant was
on bail. In all the circumstances that degree of cumulacy was
reasonably open. The resulting total effective sentence of 5 years and
4 months' immediate imprisonment bears a proper relationship to the
overall criminality involved in all of the appellant's offending, viewed
in its entirety, after having regard to all relevant facts and
circumstances and all relevant sentencing factors, including the total
effective sentences imposed in comparable cases.

67

Standing back and evaluating the total effective sentence imposed
on the appellant against the customary sentencing standards evinced by
the broadly comparable cases, it is readily apparent that the sentencing
judge imposed a sentence that was commensurate with the seriousness
of the appellant's offending, but which properly reflected his personal
mitigating factors. Those personal mitigating factors were not

In so concluding it is important to remember that, putting aside the

reduction in the sentences to be imposed, matters personal to the
appellant carry less weight in sentencing for offences of this kind.
Moreover, while there was significant mitigation in the appellant's

continued offending while on bail meant that personal deterrence
remained a factor of real significance.

The total effective sentence of 5 years and 4 months' immediate
imprisonment was within the range reasonably open to the sentencing
judge on a proper exercise of the sentencing discretion. Error should
not be implied or inferred from the sentencing outcome in relation to
the total effective sentence. The first limb of the totality principle was
not infringed.
