# Moloney v Mercer [1971] 2 NSWLR 207

## One line
Naked exposure in a public place is not automatically indecent under s 11 — the test is whether the conduct offends the ordinary modesty of the average man, judged in context.

## What happened
MERCER performed alone on stage at the Coconut Jungle Strip Palais in Kings Cross before a paying audience of about 60 people, predominantly male. She removed a G-string and stood completely naked for one to one and a half minutes. She was charged under s 11 of the Summary Offences Act 1970 (NSW) with being a person whose person was indecently exposed in a public place (a conceded public place). At the close of the prosecution case the magistrate found no case to answer and dismissed the information, reasoning that indecency required some improper or unwarranted display of, or drawing attention to, sexual organs. The informant (a police sergeant from the Special Licences Duty Section) appealed by way of case stated.

## What the Court held
TAYLOR J held the magistrate erred in point of law. It is not enough merely to prove a woman was naked in a public place, but nor is it necessary to prove any display of or drawing attention to sexual organs — that reasoning belongs to the separate obscenity offence (s 12). The correct test for indecency under s 11 is whether the conduct transgresses the generally accepted bounds of decency, i.e. whether it would offend the ordinary modesty of the average man (applying Crowe v Graham). Whether exposure crosses the line of decency is a question of fact for the tribunal and is not to be determined by expert evidence. The nature of the public place and the circumstances of the exposure are relevant factors — what offends on a crowded beach may differ from a theatre. There was evidence on which the magistrate could convict, so there was a case to answer; the case was remitted.

## What it means for your casework
This tells you how to think about indecent exposure evidence. Nakedness alone in a public place is not automatically an offence — you need to be able to show the exposure would offend ordinary community standards of modesty in the circumstances. Build your brief around the context: the nature of the place, who was present, the manner and duration of the exposure, and any surrounding circumstances that make it sordid or offensive. Do not gather expert opinion evidence to prove the exposure was indecent — the tribunal decides that itself from the facts. Note the distinction between indecent exposure and the more serious obscene exposure: obscenity involves a deliberate lewd or sexual dimension (drawing attention to genitals), whereas mere indecency sits lower on the scale. Note this is a NSW decision on NSW legislation — check the current WA offence provisions and their wording before relying on it.

MOLONEY v. MERCER
In Chambers: Taylor J.
Oct. 26; Nov. 4, 1971.
Criminal Law—Statutory offence—Indecent exposure of person in a public
place—Meaning of “indecent”—Summary Offences Act, 1970, s. 11.

M. was prosecuted for that she was a person whose person was indecently
exposed in a public place contrary to s. 11 of the Summary Offences Act,
1970. At the hearing there was evidence that M. had appeared alone on a
stage, before a paying audience composed mainly of men in a place which
was a public place for the purposes of the Act, clad only in a G-string;
and that she had removed the G-string and had stood before the audience
completely naked for a period of one to one and a half minutes. No expert
evidence was given. At the conclusion of the Crown case the magistrate
ruled that there was no case to answer and dismissed the information,
substantially on the ground that there was no evidence of improper or
unwarranted display of, or drawing of attention to, organs of sex. On appeal,
by way of case stated,
Held: (1) It is not sufficient in a prosecution under s. 11 of the Summary
Offences Act to establish only that a woman has exposed herself naked in
a public place.
(2) It is necessary to establish that the defendant's conduct has transgressed
the generally accepted bounds of decency, i.e., that it offends the ordinary
modesty of the average man.
Crowe v. Graham (1968) 41 A.L.J.R. 402, at p. 410, followed.
(3) Whether exposure transcends the limits of decency is not to be determined by the opinions of expert witnesses.
Transport Publishing Co. Pty. Ltd. v. The Literature Board of Review
(1956) 99 C.L.R. 111, and Crowe v. Graham (1968) 41 A.L.J.R. 402, at
p. 410, followed.
(4) In deciding whether the exposure of the naked human body in a
public place is indecent the nature of the public place and circumstances of
the exposure are factors to be taken into consideration.
(5) There being evidence upon which the magistrate might have convicted
the defendant, there was a case to answer and the case would be remitted to
the magistrate to be dealt with according to law.
The distinction between “indecency” and “obscenity”, considered and explained.
McGowan v. Langmuir 1931 S.C.(J.) 10 and R. v. Stanley [1965] 2 Q.B.
327, referred to.

CASE STATED.
This was an appeal from the decision of a magistrate dismissing an information alleging, under s. 11 of the Summary Offences Act, 1970, that the
respondent was a person whose person was indecently exposed in a public
place. The question asked in the case stated was whether the determination
of the magistrate acquitting the defendant was erroneous in point of law.

C. A. Porter for the appellant (informant).
P. D. White, for the respondent (defendant).

Nov. 4.
TAYLOR J. This is an appeal by way of case stated from a decision of a
magistrate dismissing an information wherein it was alleged that the respondent, Evelyn Louise Mercer, was a person whose person was indecently
exposed in a public place contrary to s. 11 of the Summary Offences Act,
1970.
The appellant, the informant before the magistrate, was a sergeant of
police attached to the Special Licences Duty Section. In the course of his
duties he visited premises at Darlinghurst Road, Kings Cross known as the
Coconut Jungle Strip Palais. According to the facts found by the learned
magistrate these premises are a public place within the meaning of the Act.
There were present on this occasion some sixty persons, predominantly
males, who had paid two dollars each for admission. The respondent appeared before this audience alone on a stage, clad only in a brief G-string.
This she removed and appeared before the audience completely naked for
a period of some one to one and a half minutes. There was no other person
on the stage; the lighting was subdued, but adequate. A musical background
was provided.
At the conclusion of the case for the informant the learned magistrate
upheld the submission for the respondent that there was no case to answer,
and accordingly dismissed the information. In so holding, the magistrate
was deciding, as a matter of law, that the respondent, on the evidence as
it then stood before him, could not lawfully be convicted: see May v.
O'Sullivan (1),. The question of law raised by the case stated is thus: Was
this determination acquitting the respondent erroneous in point of law?
The Summary Offences Act is an Act which repeals the Vagrancy Act, 1902,
together with certain provisions of the Police Offences Act, 1901, and of the
Local Government Act, 1919. It creates a number of offences and s. 11 is part
of div. 1, which deals with offences relating to public places, ss. 6-21. Section
11 provides: “A person whose person is indecently exposed in or within view
of a public place or a school is guilty of an offence. Penalty one hundred
dollars.”
(1) (1955) 92 C.L.R. 654, at p. 658.

This takes the place of s. 78 of the Police Offences Act, which was in the
following terms: “Whosoever offends against decency by the exposure of his
person in, or in view of, any street or public place shall be liable to a penalty
not exceeding one hundred dollars.”
Section 12 of the Summary Offences Act provides: “A person who, in or
within view of a public place or a school, wilfully and obscenely exposes his
person is guilty of an offence. Penalty four hundred dollars or imprisonment
for six months.”
This is a re-enactment substantially of the provisions of s. 4 (2) (d) of the
Vagrancy Act, which provided:
“Whosoever —
(d) wilfully and obscenely exposes his person in or in view of any street,
roadway or public highway or any place of public resort shall be liable to
imprisonment for a term not exceeding six months.”
The Summary Offences Act contains a definition of “public place”, but this is
not of significance, since it is conceded that the place in question here is a
public place within the meaning of that Act. It will thus be seen that the
distinction between the offence of indecently exposing a person's body and
unlawfully and obscenely exposing it is preserved in this Act and a prosecution
under s. 11 does not involve questions of obscenity. Section 11 concerns itself
with the fact of exposure, not the manner of it. The purpose of s. 11 is no
doubt plain enough. It proceeds on the basis that in public people shall be
clothed in such a fashion that their bodies will not be exposed indecently, that
is to an extent which would offend against the recognized standards of
propriety. It is no doubt designed to deal with cases where brevity of dress
could proceed to an extent at which the exposure of the body offended against
decency.
In the present case we are concerned with an exposure of the body in a
public place where the woman was naked. It is asserted by the Crown that
the complete exposure of the body in a public place, by male or female, is
necessarily an offence against this section, since the law has always regarded
as indecent the public exhibition of the naked body of a person.
In Halsbury's Laws of England, 3rd ed., vol. 10, p. 666, s. 13 dealing with
Offences against Decency and Morality, par. 1272 states: “Indecency. The
public exhibition of the naked person or any other act of open and notorious
lewdness is an indictable misdemeanour at common law. Similar exposure,
even though in a place of public resort, is not indictable at common law if it is
only visible by one person. The exposure is indictable although the place is not
one of public resort if the place is such that a number of persons can and do
see the act. Bathing in a state of nudity in a place near to which persons
frequently pass is indictable.”
I have no doubt that this is the position at common law in this country.
Mr. Porter, for the appellant, contended that in the light of this, upon it
being proved that the woman was naked and that the place was public, the
offence under s. 11 was complete. There is, I think, much to be said for
this view, but I do not think that the section is as absolute as Mr. Porter
contends. It may be that the question of the nature of the public place is an
element to be taken into consideration. What would offend against decency
in a public place such as a crowded surfing beach may not offend against

decency in a theatre or other place of entertainment. These are matters to
be determined by the tribunal which has to decide the ultimate question of
fact. I am only concerned here with the preliminary question of whether or
not there was evidence upon which the respondent could lawfully have been
convicted.
The ordinary meaning of the word “indecency” is: “unseemliness, unbecoming or outrageous conduct”. I do not think that in the field of the
criminal law it has any meaning different from this. The relationship of the
two words “indecent” and “obscene” is throughout the criminal law a close
one. The distinction is pointed out by the Court of Appeal in the case of
R. v. Stanley (2),, where it was held that “the words ‘indecent’ and ‘obscene’
convey one idea, namely, offending against the recognised standards of
propriety, indecent being at the lower and obscene at the upper end of the
scale; that an indecent article was not necessarily obscene, whereas an
obscene article almost certainly was indecent”. Lord Parker, giving the
judgment of the Court, quoted with approval the judgment of Lord Sands
in the Scottish case of McGowan v. Langmuir (3),: “I do not think that the
two words ‘indecent’ and ‘obscene’ are synonymous. The one may shade into
the other, but there is a difference of meaning. It is easier to illustrate than
define, and I illustrate thus: for a male bather to enter the water nude in
the presence of ladies would be indecent, it would not necessarily be obscene.
But if he directed the attention of a lady to a certain member of his body
his conduct would certainly be obscene.” This distinction is preserved by
s. 12 of the Summary Offences Act.
I am of opinion that the learned magistrate fell into error in this case
because he concerned himself largely with matters not relevant to the information before him. Having decided that the decision of the High Court in
Crowe v. Graham (4),, was not a precedent he should follow since, although
it was concerned with questions of indecency and obscenity, these related
to s. 16 of the Obscene and Indecent Publications Act, 1901, and were concerned with printing or publishing of obscene or indecent material, he then
proceeded to state, having referred to s. 7 of the Vagrancy Act: “I feel it is
pertinent to note here incidentally that it will probably be found that where an
act of indecency has been found to be committed there is some improper or
unwarranted display of or drawing of attention to organs of sex or acts
drawing attention to these organs.” Later in his reasons for judgment he said:
“The evidence was quite clear that the young lady's body was completely
exposed. There was no contest or argument that it was a public place. It is
simply a question in the first instance, in the prima facie sense, of whether what
was done was done indecently.” This is the reasoning that lies behind his
rejection of the case. He took the view that so long as this young woman did
no more than be naked before this audience there could not be an offence.
This is not a matter which arises under s. 11 but one which could well arise
in a prosecution under s. 12.
The learned magistrate also, in my opinion, was in error in thinking that he
should have had the assistance of some expert evidence on the matter. Ques(2) [1965] 2 Q.B. 327.

tions of whether exposure transcends the limits of indecency, like questions of
whether a book or a picture are indecent or obscene, are to be determined by
a consideration of the object itself and not by the opinions of expert witnesses. This is, I think, clear from the judgment of the High Court in Transport
Publishing Co. Pty. Ltd. v. The Literature Board of Review (5),. In Crowe v.
Graham (6), Windeyer J., when dealing with an allied subject matter, said:
“Does the publication, by reason of the extent to which and the matter in
which it deals with sexual matters, transgress the generally accepted bounds of
decency? That is a question of fact to be decided by the tribunal of fact. It is
to be answered by reading the publication. Common sense and a sense of
decency must supply the answer. Only within very narrow limits is evidence
beyond the publication itself necessary or admissible. Evidence of what has
been published in other books or writings is not admissible. The court has to
determine whether the publication before it is obscene having regard to the
persons, classes of persons and age groups to whom or amongst whom the
matter was published. The answer to that question is not to be had by a process
of literary comparison. Nor is it to be had by calling witnesses—whether
writers, publishers or psychologists—and asking them to give their opinion in
the matter. I respectfully agree with what Martin J. said in the Supreme Court
of Victoria on this aspect in Wavish v. Associated Newspapers Ltd. (7),. As
Dixon C.J., Kitto J. and Taylor J. said in this Court: ‘Once the court permits
the boundaries to be transgressed which the law places upon proof of the
opinion of others in such a matter, it is very likely to be drawn from the issue
by a flood of controversial argument as to the effect and desirability of the
publications which will be advanced in the guise of expert testimony’: Transport Publishing Co. Pty. Ltd. v. The Literature Board of Review (8),.”
In my opinion, this principle applies to the present case, and the learned
magistrate was thus in error in taking into consideration what did or did not
happen on the stage in the musical production “Hair” to which he referred. If
it was the fact that in the production of that play people appeared naked on
the stage, this is immaterial for the determination of the question which arises
in the instant case.
The magistrate was, in my opinion, entitled to take into account the nature
of the place where this offence is alleged to have been committed on the
question of whether or not exposure of the naked body was, in that place and
under those conditions, indecent.
The question of it being indecent depends upon whether or not it would
offend the ordinary modesty of the average man. This is the test that the
magistrate adopted in Crowe v. Graham (9), and it met with the approval of
the High Court. It is not, I think, a different test from that of “recognized
standards of propriety” the phrase used by the Court of Appeal in R. v.
Stanley (10),. True it is in Crowe's case the question was the indecency of a
publication, but I see no reason why the same test ought not to apply in the
present case. Offences of indecent publication, obscene publication and indecent exposure were all originally dealt with by the common law as part of the
law of public nuisance: see Russell on Crime, 11th ed., p. 1633.

At the stage when the application was made, if the material before the
magistrate was reasonably capable of offending the ordinary modesty of the
average man then there was in law a case to answer. The ordinary modesty
of the average man is to be determined by the tribunal of fact, just as the
standard of care that is to be expected of a reasonable man is determined. I
would venture to think that the average man's ordinary modesty would be
based on a respect for women and, to an extent at least, on a firmly held
conviction that in public, although they may appear briefly and scantily clad,
they do not appear naked. How then might a man of this standard of modesty
react to the spectacle that is the subject of this prosecution; that is a woman
in what I think might be fairly described as sleazy surroundings, standing
naked before a predominantly male audience who had paid to see her nakedness? I am of the opinion that he might well regard this as a dirty and sordid
affair, offensive to him; he might well feel anger, not with the woman who was
the spectacle but with those who promoted it and profited thereby.
There was, in my opinion, in this case evidence upon which the magistrate
could convict. Certainly there was a case to answer. I answer the question
posed: Yes. I remit the case to the magistrate to be dealt with in accordance
with law. The respondent is to pay the costs of this appeal.
Order accordingly.
