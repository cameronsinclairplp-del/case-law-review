REASONS OF THE COURT:
Introduction and overview

1. This interlocutory appeal arises from criminal proceedings in the District Court that have a
very sorry history.

2.

2. The appellant, Joanne Pellew, has been charged with a number of offences against the Corpora
tions Act 2001 (Cth) . While she was initially charged with 10 offences, the indictment now
alleges seven separate offences. The alleged offences relate to events in 2018 and 2019.

3. The charges against Ms Pellew were first listed for trial on 2 December 2022, with the trial
listed to commence on 6 March 2024. Shortly prior to that trial (on 26 February 2024) the
initial trial dates were vacated. The trial was relisted to commence on 5 March 2025.
4. Those trial dates were also vacated (on 26 February 2025) and the trial relisted to commence
on 13 October 2025. Prior to 13 October 2025, both the Crown and Ms Pellew filed a number
of pretrial applications (including an application pursuant to s 31A of the Evidence Act 1903
(WA) and an application to permanently stay a number of counts on the indictment).
5. On 12 September 2025, a month out from trial, Ms Pellew filed an application pursuant to s 1
33 of the Criminal Procedure Act 2004 (WA) to sever what are now counts 1 to 3 from counts
4 to 7 on the indictment.
6. The Crown contended that the joinder of all charges was permissible and that the evidence in
connection with counts 1 to 3 was admissible in proof of counts 4 to 7, but conceded that the
alleged offending charged in counts 4 to 7, alleged to have occurred later in time, was not
likewise admissible to prove counts 1 to 3.
7. The application was not able to be dealt with until the morning of trial on 13 October 2025.
8. Justice Howard refused the severance application. His Honour concluded that the evidence
intended to be led by the Crown in relation to counts 1 to 3 was admissible in relation to
Ms Pellew's state of mind concerning counts 4 to 7 and that any potential prejudice to
Ms Pellew could be guarded against by a 'properly worded and stern direction to the jury'.[1]

[1] Trial ts 13 October 2025, 274.

9. Before formally refusing the severance application, Howard J vacated the trial date (in light of
s 133(7) of the Criminal Procedure Act and s 26(5) of the Criminal Appeals Act 2004 (WA) )
. The trial is now listed to commence on 8 June 2026.
10. Ms Pellew now seeks leave to appeal from Howard J's decision to refuse the severance
application. For the reasons that follow, we would refuse leave to appeal and dismiss the
appeal. In our view, the counts on the indictment are properly joined and the learned primary
judge did not err in the exercise of his discretion under s 133 of the Criminal Procedure Act .
11. Needless to say, the interests of justice require that the proceedings below be promptly
brought to a final resolution.

It seems to me that it is clear that the circumstances which led to the incorporation of Ochre
People Proprietary Limited in August of 2018 would be relevant to the matters of the fault

submissions. I think that is equally the case for the accused's role in the incorporation of Ochre

Ochre People in August 2018 are relevant to those matters set out in paragraph 18.
The question that I think is more difficult is whether counts 1 to 3 and the evidence intended to be

time sufficiently to be probative to her state of mind as to the business of Ochre People.

40. Before addressing the ground of appeal it is convenient to briefly refer to the relevant legal
principles.
Legal principles
Joinder – legal principles
41. Schedule 1 to the Criminal Procedure Act , which applies to indictments by s 85, provides, in
cl 7(3) :
A prosecution notice or indictment may charge one or more persons with 2 or more offences if the
offences –
(a)

form or are a part of a series of offences of the same or a similar character; or

(b)

are alleged to arise substantially out of the same or closely related acts or
omissions; or

(c)

are alleged to arise from a series of acts or omissions done or omitted to be done
in the prosecution of a single purpose,

and may do so without alleging a connection between the offences.

42. The law is settled that, particularly in light of the discretion to direct separate trials in the
event of likely prejudice, the provisions of cl 7(3) should not be given an unduly restricted
meaning. [20]

[20] Zammit v The State of Western Australia [2007] WASCA 66; (2007) 34 WAR 302 [
27], [39] (Steytler P, Wheeler & Pullin JJA agreeing); Barnes v The Queen [2001]
WASCA 86 [12] (Parker J, Wallwork & Wheeler JJ agreeing).

43. Those principles were restated, in relation to cl 7(3)(a) , in HTN v The State of Western
Australia in which the court said: [21]
In order for joinder to be authorised under cl 7(3)(a) , the offences must not only be of the same or
a similar character; they must form or be part of a series of offences of the same or a similar
character.
A sufficient nexus to find the offences form or are part of a series of offences of a similar
character is established if the offences are so connected that evidence of one would be admissible
on the trial of the other, but the provision is not restricted to such cases. What is required is a
sufficient correlation to enable the offences to be described as a 'series' without straining the word
beyond the meaning which it is reasonably capable of bearing. The words 'a series of offences of
the same or a similar character', while somewhat imprecise, have been said to 'call for the
administration of a test in which time, place and other circumstances of the offences as well as
their legal character or category are all factors which are considered for the purpose of seeing
whether the necessary features of similarity and connection are present'.
…
Bearing in mind the existence of the discretion under s 133 of the CPA, provisions as to joinder
such as cl 7(3) , should not be given an unduly restricted meaning.
…
Thus the existence of the relevant nexus for the purposes of cl 7(3)(a) is a question of fact and
degree, not lending itself to precise definition. (footnotes omitted)

Severance – legal principles

[21] HTN v The State of Western Australia [2022] WASCA 51 ( HTN ) [58] - [60], [62] (Buss
P, Mazza & Beech JJA).

44. The severance application in the present case sought separate trials in relation to the different
charges under s 133(3) of the Criminal Procedure Act .
45. Section 133 of the Criminal Procedure Act relevantly provides:
(3)

If a court is satisfied that an accused is likely to be prejudiced in the trial of a
prosecution notice or indictment because it contains 2 or more charges, the court
may order –

(a)

that the accused be tried separately on one or more of the charges;
and

(b)

the prosecutor to tell the court the order in which the charges will
be tried.

…
(5)

In deciding whether to make an order under subsection (3) or (4) in respect of an
indictment to be tried by a jury, it is open to a superior court –
(a)

to decide that any likelihood of the accused being prejudiced can
be guarded against by a direction to the jury; and

(b)

to so decide irrespective of the nature of the offence or offences
charged; and

(c)

to so decide even if –
(i)

the evidence on one of the charges is inadmissible
on another; or

(ii)

the evidence against one of the accused is not
admissible against another,

as the case requires.

46. The relevant principles in the application of s 133 are also uncontentious. The relevant
'prejudice' is, of course, the prejudice that may arise from the charges being tried together. In
that context, where the evidence in relation to one charge is admissible in the case of another
charge, generally speaking the relevant prejudice will not arise. As the court observed in The
State of Western Australia v JHN , where it was concluded that evidence was
crossadmissible as between separate charges: [22]
A consequence of the crossadmissibility of the evidence is that there is no relevant impermissible
prejudice to the respondent from the joint trial of count 1 and count 2 because if there were
separate trials on count 1 and count 2 the jury in each separate trial would hear the propensity
evidence. Severance is not required to ensure that the respondent receives a fair trial. As we have
mentioned, appropriate directions by the trial judge will be sufficient to overcome or guard against
any impermissible prejudice to the respondent arising from the crossadmissibility of the evidence.
Also, as we have mentioned, the jury will be capable of following and giving effect to those
directions.

[22] The State of Western Australia v JHN [2021] WASCA 225; (2021) 59
WAR 143 ( JHN ) [142] (Buss P, Mazza & Vaughan JJA).

47. As their Honours here concluded, and as contemplated by s 133(5) of the Criminal Procedure
Act , relevant to the exercise of the discretion to order separate trials, will be the extent to

which any prejudice can be guarded against by directions to the jury. In JHN , for example,
their Honours observed that: [23]
(a)

The jury will be instructed that the counts on the indictment are separate charges
which require separate consideration and separate verdicts.

(b)

The jury will be instructed that the verdict on one count does not need to be the
same as the verdict on the other count.

(c)

The jury will be instructed that no adverse inference may be drawn against the
respondent arising from the indictment containing more than one count.

(d)

The jury will be instructed as to the use which the jury may make (and must not
make) of the propensity evidence adduced on each count.

(e)

The jury will be instructed not to reason that the respondent is guilty of a count
merely because the jury might form the view that he was a person likely to do the
charged acts.

(f)

The jury will be instructed that it is irrelevant to proof of the charged offences
that the jury might form the view that the respondent has escaped punishment for
other offences.

(g)

The jury will be instructed that it must not convict the respondent of either of the
counts unless the State has proved beyond reasonable doubt each of the legal and
factual elements of the count the jury is considering.

[23] JHN [133] (Buss P, Mazza & Vaughan JJA).

48. While these proposed directions were made in the context of evidence 'crossadmissible' as
propensity evidence (see par (d)), they would also be applicable, with necessary
modifications, in relation to evidence that was admissible in relation to each of the charges on
some other basis.
49. In that context, of course, evidence relevant to one charge may be admissible, at common law,
in relation to another charge or charges for reasons that do not involve any tendency or
propensity reasoning. As Brennan J said in Harriman v The Queen : [24]
If, as a matter of human experience, the evidence tends to establish the offence charged or a fact in
issue otherwise than by showing merely the commission of another offence or a propensity to
commit an offence, the evidence is admissible.

[24] Harriman v The Queen [1989] HCA 50; (1989) 167 CLR 590, 594
(Brennan J).

50. Evidence compendiously described as 'context' evidence may fall into this category of
admissible evidence, although care is always required to identify the particular basis upon
which the 'context' evidence is said to be relevant. As Quinlan CJ said in relation to 'context'
evidence in Hill v The State of Western Australia : [25]
First, as the High Court made clear in Johnson v The Queen , there are a variety of uses that fall
within the umbrella of 'contextual' uses (such as explaining a complainant's failure to complain, or
an accused's apparent confidence in acting a particular way etc). Each of these forms of 'context'
evidence involves different processes of reasoning. What they all have in common, however, is
that they are adduced only to assist in evaluating the evidence of the charged offence itself. Unlike
propensity evidence, 'context' evidence does not, of itself, increase the likelihood that the accused
committed the offence (and it may not be so used).
Secondly, and for this reason, it is generally insufficient to direct a jury that they may use
evidence of uncharged acts as 'context', without identifying the particular use that is permitted in
the instant case. To describe evidence simply as 'context' may well convey a particular meaning to
judges and lawyers. It is unlikely to assist a jury as to the process of reasoning that they are
permitted to employ in relation to the particular evidence before them. For this reason, the
direction in relation to 'context' evidence should relate to the particular permissible use in the
concrete circumstances of the particular case. (footnotes omitted)

[25] Hill v The State of Western Australia [2019] WASCA 209 [9] [10]
(Quinlan CJ).

51. Similarly evidence of previous charged or uncharged offences may be relevant to an accused's
state of mind on a subsequent charge. In that regard, evidence that may provide the basis for a
conclusion or inference that, at a particular point in time, a person held a particular state of
mind is not the same as evidence that a person had a tendency to hold a particular state of
mind or to act in a certain way.
52. For example, in Ivanoff v The Queen ,[26] Weinberg JA explained that where 'evidence of
prior exposure to the criminal law is relevant to the question [of] whether the accused has a
particular state of mind or understanding which constitutes an element of the crime charged', it
was the knowledge gained from the past misconduct that was relevant and not any tendency
on the part of the accused to engage in the conduct alleged. [27] In Ivanoff v The Queen , for
example, there was an issue as to whether the accused knew that the number of cannabis
plants he was growing exceeded a commercial quantity. The accused had some years earlier
been convicted of trafficking in cannabis. The knowledge previously acquired as a drug dealer
was relevant and admissible to prove his actual state of mind about the quantity of plants he
had cultivated.

[26] Ivanoff v The Queen [2015] VSCA 116.

[27] Ivanoff v The Queen [2015] VSCA 116 [19] (Weinberg JA).

Disposition
53. For the reasons that follow, in our view, the counts on the indictment are properly joined and
the learned primary judge did not err in the exercise of his discretion under s 133 of the Crimi
nal Procedure Act .
54. Commencing with the power of joinder under cl 7(3)(a) of sch 1 to the Criminal Procedure
Act , there is, in our assessment, a sufficient nexus between all of the charges such that they
can be properly characterised as 'part of a series of offences of the same or a similar character'.
55. Certainly counts 1 to 3 do not have the same, or at least precisely the same, legal character as
counts 4 to 7. As the authorities make clear, however, the legal character of the charges is
only one of the factors relevant to whether there is a sufficient similarity between the various
charges. That issue is determined by reference to the factual connection between the charges,
in relation, for example, to time, place and circumstance.
56. In the present case it is clear, in our assessment, that there is a common 'substratum of fact'
underlying the entirety of the Crown case: namely Ms Pellew's role as the 'driving force
behind the 'Ochre' brand labour hire business',[28] which, on the Crown case, she simply
transferred from one corporate entity under her control to another, as she saw fit. In that
regard it is the Crown case that there was but a single 'business' which was operated
successively by Ochre Training, Ochre Workforce and Ochre People. And, relevant to her
state of mind, Ms Pellew dealt with the various companies on the basis that it was, in essence,
'her business'.

[28] Revised Outline of Crown Case [33]; BGAB 84.

57. Evidence which tends to establish this underlying substratum of fact is likely to be probative
of the facts in issue in relation to each of the charges in the indictment. It is clearly relevant to
establishing the Crown's case as to Ms Pellew's purpose in authorising the transfers the subject
of counts 1 to 3 and, in turn, to establishing the Crown's case that she did so 'dishonestly' (that
is, 'dishonest' according to the standards of ordinary, decent people).
58. That evidence, however, would also be relevant to establishing the nature of Ochre People's
business (in relation to counts 4 to 7) and, significantly, Ms Pellew's role in that business. If,
as the Crown seeks to prove, Ms Pellew at all times regarded herself as the 'driving force'
behind a single business operated by each of Ochre Training, Ochre Workforce and Ochre
People and that her role remained essentially unchanged over the entirety of the period
covered by the indictment (notwithstanding that she became disqualified from managing
corporations on 20 February 2019), that intermediate fact would be highly relevant to a jury's
assessment as to whether she was reckless as to whether she participated in decisions affecting
the whole or a substantial part of Ochre People's business.

59. Accordingly, and contrary to grounds of appeal 1(a) and 1(c), we agree with the learned
primary judge's assessment that the evidence relevant to the fact of, and the purpose of, the
transfers the subject of counts 1 to 3 would be admissible in relation to Ms Pellew's state of
mind in relation to counts 4 to 7. That 'crossadmissibility' is sufficient to support the joinder of
the counts on the indictment. As the authorities make clear, a sufficient nexus is established if
the offences are so connected that evidence of one would be admissible on the trial of the
other. [29]

[29] See HTN [59] (Buss P, Mazza & Beech JJA); [43] above.

60. Moreover, Ms Pellew's position, as articulated in the appeal, reinforces, rather than refutes,
the commonality of the evidence relevant to each of the charges. Ms Pellew's position in
relation to counts 1 to 3, that, contrary to the Crown's case, Ochre Workforce in fact
performed work for Ochre Training prior to the transfers the subject of those counts and that it
was owed money by Ochre Training, underscores the relevance of the range of evidence relied
upon by the Crown in support of its case to the contrary.
61. Even more significantly, Ms Pellew's contention in relation to counts 4 to 7, that the business
of Ochre People involved 'an entirely different type of business' to that conducted by Ochre
Training and Ochre Workforce, confirms that whether the business of each corporation was
the same and whether Ms Pellew's role in the management of each corporation was the same,
is a matter that will be the subject of significant contest at trial, in relation to which evidence
as to the business conducted by each corporation, and Ms Pellew's role in their management,
will be crucial.
62. For these reasons the counts are properly joined (contrary to ground of appeal 1(b)).
63. The conclusion that evidence in relation to counts 1 to 3 was relevant to counts 4 to 7 also
largely answers Ms Pellew's position in relation to prejudice. As the Court observed in JHN ,
the effect of the crossadmissibility of the evidence in relation to counts 1 to 3 for counts 4 to 7
is such that there is no relevant impermissible prejudice to the appellant from a joint trial
because, if there was a separate trial on counts 4 to 7, the jury in that trial would hear the
evidence in relation to counts 1 to 3 in any event.
64. Ms Pellew submits that the learned primary judge, however, erred because his Honour did not
take into account the prejudice that might result from the jury hearing evidence in relation to
counts 4 to 7 in its consideration of counts 1 to 3.
65. We do not consider that his Honour made such an error. While it is correct that the learned
primary judge focused on the admissibility of the evidence relevant to counts 1 to 3 in relation
to counts 4 to 7, that is because the application before him was argued on that basis. The
Crown conceded that the evidence of alleged offending in counts 4 to 7 was not admissible to
prove the allegations in counts 1 to 3. Nevertheless, when addressing prejudice his Honour did
not confine his conclusions to prejudice operating in any particular 'direction'. His Honour
referred to 'the prejudice, which might be said to arise from the combined trial'. It was that

potential prejudice that his Honour concluded could be guarded against by an appropriate
direction (contrary to ground of appeal 1(d)).
66. In our view, his Honour has not been shown to be in error. Indeed, in our view, his Honour
was correct (contrary to ground of appeal 1(e)).
67. The principal prejudice that might arise by the joint trial of all of the charges in the indictment
in this case, is the potential prejudice from the evidence led to prove that Ms Pellew
'dishonestly' made the transfers in counts 1 to 3. In considering those charges, the jury will be
required to make an assessment, according to the standards of ordinary, decent people, as to
whether in all of the circumstances Ms Pellew's conduct was dishonest. The prejudice to be
guarded against in those circumstances is the prejudice that a jury might reason that, because
she had been 'dishonest' on one or more occasions, that Ms Pellew was thereby guilty of the
other charges. This is how we understand the learned primary judge's observation to the effect
that, while the evidence in relation to counts 1 to 3 is admissible in relation to Ms Pellew's
state of mind in relation to counts 4 to 7, 'the allegations of dishonesty, which are made in
counts 1 to 3' are not. What his Honour was there making clear is that the admissibility of the
evidence in relation to counts 1 to 3 to the proof of counts 4 to 7 did not admit of any
propensity reasoning.
68. For these reasons, it would be necessary (in addition to the general directions referred to in JHN
) [30] to instruct the jury in this case not to reason that, because they might have concluded
that Ms Pellew was 'dishonest' in relation to one count, that it is more likely that she is guilty
of another count and, that they must consider each count separately, and must not convict
Ms Pellew of any charge unless the Crown has proved beyond reasonable doubt each of the
legal and factual elements of the count the jury is considering.

[30] JHN [133] (Buss P, Mazza & Vaughan JJA); [47] above.

69. In our view, no similar 'prejudice' arises from the jury hearing evidence in relation to counts 4
to 7 in its consideration of counts 1 to 3. Given the Crown's concession that the evidence of
alleged offending in counts 4 to 7 does not go to prove the alleged offending in counts 1 to 3,
in any separate trial of counts 1 to 3, the jury would not ordinarily hear evidence of those
subsequent events. In this trial, the jury will hear evidence of both sets of charges.
70. Counts 4 to 7, however, involve no element of dishonesty. And while the evidence of past
events is relevant to the alleged offending in counts 4 to 7 as explained above, a properly
instructed jury could find Ms Pellew guilty of managing a corporation while disqualified from
so doing, but not those offences alleged in counts 1 to 3, and vice versa. In those
circumstances, the prospect that a jury would reason that, because they had concluded that
Ms Pellew was reckless as to whether the decisions she participated in affected the whole or a
significant part of the business of Ochre People, that she had been dishonest in relation to the
transfers the subject of counts 1 to 3, is in our view speculative.

71.

71. Nevertheless, it would be appropriate for the trial judge in the present case to direct the jury
that the alleged offending conduct the subject of counts 4 to 7 was irrelevant to, and should
form no part of, its consideration of counts 1 to 3.

72. Such a direction does not involve the jury engaging in mental gymnastics. In counts 1 to 3, the
relevant timeframe during which the jury will be asked to assess Ms Pellew's use of her
position as a director of a corporation and whether that use was dishonest and accompanied by
an intention of gaining an advantage for another, is at the time of having authorised the
transfer alleged in each count, at which time the conduct alleged in each of counts 4 to 7 had
not yet occurred and would not occur until the following year. It should be possible for the
trial judge to craft a clear direction which both directs the jury in terms of [71] above, and
reinforces the direction with the logic of limiting the jury's consideration to events and
knowledge which could have impacted upon Ms Pellew's state of mind at the relevant time.
73. There is no reason to suppose that the jury would not be capable of following and giving
effect to any of the directions we have referred to above. Indeed there is every reason to
conclude that it would. In that regard, the allegations in the present case, while clearly
involving allegations of dishonesty and other improper conduct, are nevertheless confined to
commercial dealings. Juries are routinely capable of following and giving effect to directions
not to reason from their conclusions in relation to one charge that an accused is guilty of other
charges in cases alleging sexual or physical violence, where the evidence can engender
visceral reactions or moral revulsion. A fortiori, a jury would be capable of following such
directions when faced with allegations of breaches of the Corporations Act , even allegations
of dishonesty.
Conclusion
74. Leave to appeal should be refused. The appeal must be dismissed.
