MCGRATH J:

	On 2 July 2026, the State of Western Australia applied for a restriction order in respect of the respondent under s 48 of the High Risk Serious Offenders Act 2020 (WA) (HRSO Act).
	The respondent is currently a sentenced prisoner who is due to be released from custody on 28 November 2026.
	The preliminary hearing application came before me on 28 August 2026. At the hearing, the respondent conceded that there were reasonable grounds for believing that the court might find that he is a high risk serious offender within the meaning of the HRSO Act.  I accept that the concession was properly made and conclude that there are reasonable grounds to believe that the court might find that the respondent is a high risk serious offender.
	The State submitted that the respondent should be detained subject to an interim detention order pending the determination of the restriction order application.
	I am satisfied that the respondent should be subject to an interim detention order until the determination of the restriction order or until further order of the court.
The law
	Section 46(1) of the HRSO Act provides that the main purpose of the preliminary hearing is to decide whether the court is satisfied that there are reasonable grounds for believing that the court might find that the respondent is a high risk serious offender within the meaning of the HRSO Act.
	A 'high risk serious offender' is defined by s 7(1) of the HRSO Act as a person in relation to whom the court is satisfied, by acceptable and cogent evidence, and to a high degree of probability, that it is necessary to make a restriction order to ensure adequate protection of the community against an unacceptable risk that the person will commit a serious offence.
	A 'serious offence' within the meaning of the HRSO Act is defined in s 5 and sch 1.  Relevantly, a 'serious offence' includes the offence of aggravated robbery.
	I do not have to be satisfied that a restriction order will be made at the substantive hearing.  It is sufficient if there are reasonable grounds for believing that an order might be made.  To say that something might occur is to say that it is possible, and belief is an inclination of mind towards assenting to, rather than rejecting, a proposition.  For there to be reasonable grounds for belief therefore requires the existence of facts which are sufficient to induce that state of mind in a reasonable person.
	Under s 35(1) of the HRSO Act, the State may apply for a restriction order in relation to a serious offender 'under custodial sentence' who is not a serious offender under restriction, where there is a possibility that the offender might be released from custody within the period of one year after the application is made.
	Under s 3 of the HRSO Act, a 'serious offender under custodial sentence' includes a person who is under custodial sentence for offences other than a serious offence and has been under that sentence at all times since being discharged from a custodial sentence for a serious offence.
The evidence
	I have received three affidavits filed by the applicant, namely:
	•	Affidavit of Mr McDonnell, legal practitioner, sworn 9 July 2026 and annexures thereto comprising the respondent's criminal history and a number of reports, including psychiatric and psychological assessments, and further, treatment reports regarding programs undertaken by the respondent.
	•	Affidavit of Mr Kirk, Detective Senior Sergeant, sworn 16 July 2026, producing information relevant to accommodation.
	•	Affidavit of Ms Cumming, Senior Community Corrections Officer, affirmed 8 July 2026, addressing accommodation issues.
Antecedents
	The respondent is 38 years of age having been born on 17 April 1988. The respondent is the only child from his parents' union but has four younger halfsiblings from his mother's other unions and five younger halfsiblings from his father's other unions. The respondent reported that he has no connection to his half-siblings.
	The respondent reported that his father had substance abuse issues and his mother was violent towards him and neglected him.  Consequently, the respondent grew up with his grandparents until his grandfather passed away when he was 8 years old, after which time the respondent was placed in foster care. The respondent remained in foster care until he was 13 years of age and thereafter lived with various relatives. The respondent reported his experience of foster care as difficult and perceives his childhood experiences as the cause for his anger.
	The respondent reported that he commenced smoking cannabis and drinking alcohol when he was 11 years of age and commenced using speed and cocaine when he was 18 years of age and experimented with LSD and MDMA.
	The respondent attended schooling until Year 8 at which time he was removed from school due to behavioural difficulties. The respondent has undertaken further studies towards a Diploma in Chemistry. He has previously held employment as a laboratory assistant and as a plasterer. Prior to his current period of custody, he was engaged in FIFO employment.
	The respondent has been involved in two long-term relationships. The first commenced when he was around 18 years of age and lasted for five to six years.  Both relationships were characterised by domestically violent behaviours, with the respondent's behaviour in the first relationship giving rise to several domestically violent charges.
	The respondent has two young children with whom he has limited contact.
Criminal record
	The respondent has committed a significant number of offences. The respondent's offending history is outlined in his criminal record which forms part of the material relied upon by the State.  The offending history is summarised, in part, in a chronology of offending which was received in evidence.
	Index Offences
	The respondent is currently serving a term of 8 years 6 months' imprisonment imposed on 6 May 2020 with the sentence backdated to 29 May 2018.
	The respondent was convicted and sentenced for five offences, namely:
	•	Aggravated armed robbery contrary to s 392(c)  (d) of the Criminal Code (WA) (count one).
	•	Armed robbery contrary to s 392(c) of the Criminal Code (count two).
	•	Acts intended to cause grievous bodily harm or prevent arrest contrary to s 294 of the Criminal Code (count three).
	•	Being armed or pretending to be armed in a way that may cause fear contrary to s 68(1) of the Criminal Code (count four).
	•	Two counts of possessing an unlicensed firearm/ammunition contrary to s 19(1)(c) of the Criminal Code (counts five and six).
	Counts one to three are serious offences under the HRSO Act.
	Count one involved the respondent pointing a shotgun at the victim's head and stealing $35 from his wallet. The respondent was sentenced to 2 years' immediate imprisonment.
	Count two involved the respondent, on the same date and within the same factual circumstances as count one, firing a single shot at the vehicle of a friend of the victim of the first count.  The round from the shotgun pierced the rear driver's side window of the car and struck the driver's side headrest.  This traversing caused a number of the shotgun pellets to deflect, a number of which then hit the driver down the right side of his body to the arms, face and torso.
	The driver was subsequently taken to hospital where a number of the pellets were removed.  The remaining pellets were left in his skin and gradually fell out over the coming weeks and months.  The respondent was sentenced to 2 years and 6 months' immediate imprisonment.
	Count three involved the respondent on a different date to counts one and two, entering a home through unknown means.  The occupants of the home were known to the respondent.  The respondent was armed with a shotgun and was wearing a mask and gloves.  The respondent began searching the bedroom of one of the occupants, making demands for the return of a watch belonging to the respondent's grandfather.  The respondent stole items including four to five mobile phones, a laptop, a tablet, and a wallet.
	The occupant's dogs then became agitated and the respondent made threats about shooting the dogs. The police arrived and the respondent fled the scene with a bag containing the occupant's property. The respondent was sentenced to 4 years' immediate imprisonment.
Other serious offences
	The respondent has committed two other serious offences as defined in s 5(6) of the HRSO Act, namely robbery contrary to s 392(e) of the Criminal Code and lit fire likely to injure or damage contrary to s 32 of the Bush Fires Act 1954 (WA).  These charges were dealt with in the Children's Court and the respondent was sentenced to 8 months' detention and a youth community-based order, respectively.
Other offences
	The respondent has a long history of violent offences spanning from 2007 to 2024, including:
	•	eight charges of aggravated burglary and commit offence in dwelling offences contrary to s 401(2)(a) of the Criminal Code;
	•	three charges of aggravated burglary with intent in dwelling, contrary to s 401(1)(a) of the Criminal Code;
	•	two charges of threats to kill contrary to s 338B(a) of the Criminal Code;
	•	three charges of threats to injure, endanger or harm any person contrary to s 338B(b) of the Criminal Code; and
	•	six charges of assaulting a public officer contrary to s 318(1)(d) of the Criminal Code.
	The respondent has also been convicted of two charges of breach of protective bail conditions contrary to s 51(2a) of the Bail Act 1982 (WA) and two charges of breach of violence restraining orders contrary to s 61(1) of the Restraining Orders Act 1997 (WA).
Reports and assessments
	I have received a number of reports from both psychiatrists and psychologists concerning the respondent, including relevantly the psychiatric report of Dr Hall, Consultant Psychiatrist, dated 1 November 2008, psychological report of Dr Barbas, Clinical and Forensic Psychologist, dated 12 November 2019, the psychiatric report of Professor Janca, Consultant Psychiatrist, dated 29 March 2020, and the psychological report of Ms Oliveri, Clinical and Forensic Psychologist, dated 13 April 2020.
	Ms Oliveri, Clinical and Forensic Psychologist, prepared a psychological report in relation to the sentencing for the index offences.  Ms Oliveri stated that the respondent has a propensity for violence and that the index offending is an indication that the respondent is unable to make or sustain positive change. The psychologist stated that the respondent has difficulties with emotional regulation, coping, impulse control, problem solving and conflict resolution, hypersensitivity and hypervigilance to threat and poor consequential thinking. The psychologist completed the MCMIIII, a measure of personality factors and stated that persons who respond in a similar manner to the respondent display paranoia, depressive, selfdefeating, schizotypal and schizoid personality traits.  Ms Oliveri stated the respondent justified his actions, minimised the seriousness of the behaviour and expressed no remorse for his offending (his only regret extended to the impact of the offending on himself).
	Ms Oliveri administered the HCR20 v3 risk assessment protocol and assessed the respondent to be at a high risk of future violence.
	Professor Janca, Consultant Psychiatrist, used the DSM5 diagnostic system and made a diagnosis of Cluster Personality Disorder (primarily antisocial).  Professor Janca also opined that the respondent suffers from borderline and histrionic personality traits that contribute to the respondent's overall attitude and behaviour.
	Professor Janca expressed the opinion that the respondent's offending appears to be related to his personality disorder at the time of committing the index offences.
	Professor Janca stated that the respondent was in the range of high to very high risk of being charged for or convicted of another similar offence in the future.
	Professor Janca said that the respondent requires prolonged psychiatric and psychological treatment including anger management and recommended that he be referred for mental health treatment and rehabilitation to address childhood traumas, inclination to use cannabis and a reaction to frustration with violent behaviour regardless of the consequences.
	Dr Hall, Consultant Psychiatrist, in his report stated that he was unable to diagnose that the respondent had a major mental illness and diagnosed him with Cannabis Abuse and Antisocial Personality Disorder.  Dr Hall assessed the respondent's risk of future violence using the HCR20 and determined that the respondent was at moderate to high risk of future violence.  Dr Hall expressed that opinion having regard to the history of previous violence, the history of substance abuse, early maladaptive behaviours, the diagnosis of Antisocial Personality Disorder and the negative attitudes with respect to the denial or responsibility for the respondent's behaviour and its consequences.
	Dr Barbas, Clinical and Forensic Psychologist, expressed the opinion that the respondent has historically had personality issues concerning suicidal ideation, antisocial tendencies, under controlled aggression, relationship difficulties, trauma symptoms, hypervigilance, impulsivity, paranoia and narcissistic traits.  Dr Barbas stated that the respondent had participated in 13 individual psychological intervention sessions between July and October 2017 and that the respondent demonstrated limited treatment gains with particular deficits noted in his capacity for selfreflection, insight, interest and commitment to behavioural change.
	Dr Barbas noted that during this current period of remand (at that time 17 months) he had been involved in nine physically or verbally violent incidents which resulted in loss of privileges or gratuities.
Treatment undertaken
	The respondent has completed a number of programs comprising the Pathways Program, the Indigenous Family Violence Program (IFV Program) and the Cognitive Brief Intervention Program (CBI Program).
	The respondent commenced the Pathways Program on 9 November 2009. The Pathways Program is a 100hour structured program which attends to both personal circumstances and interpersonal processes that lead to criminal conduct and substance abuse.
	The respondent's engagement in the program was sporadic, having attended eight of 25 program days before being removed from the program. As a result of his non-completion of the program, the respondent's treatment needs, namely understanding of substance use and offending pathways, development of communication, problem solving and conflict resolution skills, development of interpersonal skills including victim empathy, and emotional recognition and management, remained outstanding.
	The respondent undertook the IFV Program from 12 April 2010 to 20 May 2010.  The IFV Program is a low-intensity intervention designed to address violence within Indigenous families.
	It is reported that the respondent met most program objectives, however he made minimal treatment gains.  Whilst the respondent was willing to accept responsibility for his family violence and indicated he would minimise his alcohol and drug use in the future, he indicated an intention to continue his 'selfemployment' of selling illicit substances upon his release from custody.
	It was reported that the respondent possesses the cognitive skills required to distinguish between right and wrong. However, the respondent's decisions and embedded belief system with respect to monetary rewards made it impossible for him to shift at that time.  As a result, the respondent was deemed likely to re-offend.
	The respondent undertook the CBI Program between 12 October 2011 and 20 October 2011. The program comprised eight sessions totalling 20 hours, and the respondent attended each session.
	The respondent was reported to have participated well throughout the program. The respondent showed some understanding of consequences and perspective taking and presented a basic relapse prevention plan. Further, the respondent was able to provide examples of his lack of selfcontrol and impulsivity and demonstrated adequate critical reasoning and problem-solving skills.
Whether there are reasonable grounds for believing that the respondent might be a high risk serious offender
	The respondent's antecedents and criminal record, together with the assessments of the experts, supports my finding that the court might find there is a high risk that the respondent may commit a serious offence if he is not subject to a restriction order.
	Having regard to the materials that the applicant has filed, I am satisfied that there are reasonable grounds for believing the court might find that the respondent is a high risk serious offender.
Interim detention order/interim supervision order
	The State seeks an interim detention order pursuant to s 46(2)(c)(i) of the HRSO Act in order to adequately protect the community from the risk of serious offending on the part of the respondent pending the final determination of the application.
	To make a detention order, I must be positively satisfied that such an order is appropriate, and that the protection of the community requires that the respondent be held in detention pending the full assessment required by the HRSO Act.
	I am satisfied that there is an unacceptable risk the respondent may reoffend if he is released before the hearing of the application. I am satisfied that this risk cannot be sufficiently reduced by the imposition of an interim supervision order.
	Therefore, the respondent will be detained in custody until the final hearing of the application, or until further order of the court, to protect the community from the unacceptable risk of the respondent committing a serious offence.  I have adjourned the application for an interim supervision order sine die.  I did so in case the circumstances of the respondent change prior to the restriction order hearing.
Conclusion
	I make the following orders:
	•	The hearing of the restriction order application pursuant to section 48 of High Risk Serious Offenders Act 2020 be heard on Thursday, 28 January 2027.
2.	The Respondent undergo examinations by two qualified experts, namely one psychiatrist, Professor Natalie Pyszora, and one psychologist, Dr Tara Yewers, for the purposes of preparing reports as required by sections 46(2)(a) and 74 of the High Risk Serious Offenders Act 2020 that are to be used on the hearing of the restriction order application.
3.	The experts named in order 2 are not to include in their reports, information or opinions about the Respondent based on a communication with a third person unless details of that communication sufficient to identify the person with whom the communication was held, its date and a summary of its content are included in the expert's report.
4.	The reports of the experts be provided to the Applicant at least 49 days prior to the hearing of the restriction order application.
5.	Any Treatment Options Report authored by the Department of Justice be provided to the Applicant at least 42 days prior to the date of the hearing of the restriction order application.
6.	Any Community Supervision Assessment Report authored by the Department of Justice be provided to the Applicant at least 35 days prior to the date of the hearing of the restriction order application.
7.	The experts named in order 2 liaise with the Department of Justice as to a Management Plan (if appropriate) for the Respondent to be supervised in the community.
8.	Pursuant to section 122 of the Criminal Investigation Act 2006, the experts named in order 2 may be supplied with and may view any audio-visual recordings of interviews with the Respondent, or transcripts of the same, for the purpose of preparing their reports.
9.	At the hearing of the restriction order application, the reports provided by the experts named in order 2 shall stand as the evidenceinchief of the experts and no further evidenceinchief may be adduced without the leave of the Court.
10.	The Respondent be detained in custody until the final determination of the application or until further order of the Court, pursuant to section 46(2)(c)(i) of the High Risk Serious Offenders Act 2020.
11.	The application for the interim supervision order is adjourned sine die.
12.	There be liberty to the parties to apply generally.
