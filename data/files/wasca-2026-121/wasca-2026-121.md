# The State of Western Australia v Matanga [2026] WASCA 121

## One line
Harsh conditions generally experienced by remand prisoners at Hakea are not a mitigating factor without evidence of hardship personal to the offender, and childhood deprivation only reduces moral culpability if it impairs capacity to behave lawfully.

## What happened
MATANGA, aged 25, pleaded guilty to five counts of sexual penetration of a child aged 13-16 (contrary to Criminal Code (WA) s 321(2)) involving a 14-year-old complainant on three occasions in late 2024/early 2025 in a country town. The pair met at a party, exchanged Snapchat messages, and engaged in sexual activity in his car at a secluded farming property. He knew her true age at the time of counts 2-5. Three occasions involved unprotected penile-vaginal penetration. He was arrested 07/02/2025, bailed, then remanded from 20/10/2025 on a separate pending charge. The District Court (BLACK DCJ) imposed a total effective 3 years 6 months, conditionally suspended for 2 years, treating his remand conditions at Hakea and 'Bugmy principles' as mitigating. The State appealed.

## What the Court held
Grounds 1 and 2 established. The sentencing judge erred in fact and law by treating the respondent's remand conditions as a mitigating factor. There was no evidence his conditions were materially more arduous than those of the general prison population; indeed he spent most of his remand (137 days) at Albany, not Hakea (91 days), contrary to the judge's assumption. The Court held that harsh conditions generally experienced by remand prisoners at Hakea are not a mitigating factor — those conditions reflect the general prison population, and concern about them is a matter for the executive (Prisons Act 1981 (WA) s 7) and administrative law remedies, not sentencing. For prison hardship to mitigate, an offender must adduce evidence of hardship personal to them that is more onerous than for the ordinary prisoner. On Bugmy (grounds 3-4, not decided), the Court observed a deprived childhood only reduces moral culpability where it impairs the capacity to behave lawfully; here it merely influenced poor choices and did not impair that capacity. The Court resentenced to the same terms but as immediate imprisonment, backdated to 20/10/2025, with parole eligibility.

## What it means for your casework
This decision is awareness-level for your casework but useful context on sentencing. When a court considers prison conditions in sentencing, the point of principle is that generalised Hakea conditions are not mitigating — only evidence of hardship personal to the offender counts. For serious child sexual offending under s 321(2), immediate imprisonment remains the usual and expected disposition, even where the child instigated the activity, there was no coercion, and the offender genuinely believed there was a relationship. Note the practical points relevant to your briefs: a good, thorough interview capturing admissions (here the offender admitted knowing her age) and evidence of the offender's knowledge of age (text messages about waiting until she was 18) can be decisive. The 'element of abuse' — harm, exploitation or mistreatment — is the key touchstone; document the age gap, lack of protection, and victim impact fully.

## Verdict
AWARENESS — a sentencing appeal clarifying that generalised Hakea remand conditions do not mitigate; it does not change how you investigate or charge.

## Flags (verify before relying)
- Grounds 3, 4 and 5 were not determined by the Court, as ground 1 was sufficient to require resentencing.
- Section 87 of the Sentencing Act referenced in the judgment relates to remand time; note the respondent was remanded on a separate pending charge, not the indicted offences.

42

The respondent was not held on remand for the indicted offences
for which he was sentenced. However, under s 87(1) of the Sentencing
Act, the court could take the time spent on remand into account as the
respondent had spent time on remand for another offence while on bail
for the offences for which he was being sentenced. It was open to her
Honour to take the time on remand for the other offence into account in
sentencing the respondent for the indicted offences. This could be done

Section 87 does not apply where a conditionally suspended

In recent cases there has been a tendency for counsel for offenders
to rely on arduous conditions in which prisoners have been kept on
remand at Hakea Prison as a mitigating factor. The conditions described
often include extensive lockdowns and three prisoners being allocated to
a small cell, requiring at least one of them to sleep on a mattress on the
floor of a cell, with consequent hygiene and pest issues. While these
issues are concerning and should be the subject of urgent attention by
prison authorities, they do not generally operate as a mitigating
circumstance.

45

serve at least part of a term of imprisonment in conditions that are
materially more arduous than those applicable to the general prison
population is a factor that may be taken into account in the determination

decisions of this court, the harsh conditions experienced generally by

The significance of a deprived childhood34
59

In Bugmy, the High Court recognised that an offender's deprived
childhood can impact on the sentencing process in a number of different
ways. The principles that emerge from Bugmy were discussed by this
court in SYO v The State of Western Australia.35 Without purporting to
set out an exhaustive list, the court identified that an offender's deprived
childhood may be relevant to sentencing in the following ways:36
1.

It may be relevant to the determination of a just sentence because
it is causally connected to the offending.

2.

It may be relevant, as a mitigating factor, because the offender
has managed to overcome a disadvantaged background, and the
offence committed was out of character.

3.

It may help explain, or place into context, a pattern of offending
and assist the assessment of the risk of re-offending, prospects
for rehabilitation, and any issues that may need to be addressed
if rehabilitation efforts are to succeed.

4.

It may, in a particular case, bear upon the question of whether the
offender's time in custody will be more onerous than might
otherwise be the case.
