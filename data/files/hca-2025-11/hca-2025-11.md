# The King v Ryan Churchill (a pseudonym) [2025] HCA 11

## One line
Distress evidence from a complainant is ordinary circumstantial evidence — no special jury warning about causal link or "little weight" is required under the Victorian statutory scheme.

## What happened
The respondent, RYAN CHURCHILL (a pseudonym), was convicted in the County Court of Victoria in September 2022 of two counts of incest against his stepdaughter, alleged to have occurred in 2005 when she was 13 or 14. Both the complainant and her mother gave evidence that the complainant was extremely distressed (described as a "meltdown") when she first complained of being raped by the respondent at the Children's Court in 2006 or 2007. The prosecution relied on this pre-trial distress to support the complainant's account. The trial judge gave a general circumstantial evidence direction using the distress as a worked example, but did not warn the jury about needing to find a causal link between the distress and the offending, nor that distress evidence generally carries little weight. The Court of Appeal quashed the convictions, holding those directions were required and their absence caused a substantial miscarriage of justice. The Crown appealed to the High Court.

## What the Court held
The High Court unanimously allowed the appeal and restored the convictions. Evidence of a complainant's pre-trial distress is ordinarily relevant and admissible under ss 55 and 56 of the Evidence Act 2008 (Vic), on either or both bases: supporting the complainant's credit (if the jury finds a causal link between distress and the making of the complaint) and supporting that the offending occurred (if the jury finds a causal link between distress and the offending). Weight and acceptance are matters for the jury. Pre-trial distress is not "evidence of a kind that may be unreliable" under s 31 of the Jury Directions Act 2015 (Vic), so no unreliability warning can be requested for it as a class. A trial judge is not required to warn the jury that (1) it must find a causal link before using the evidence, or (2) that distress evidence generally carries little weight. A general direction on direct/indirect (circumstantial) evidence suffices. The Court of Appeal (and the earlier decisions in Paull, Seccull and Nimely) erred by importing abolished common law rules — corroboration (swept away by s 164 Evidence Act), intermediate-fact requirements (ss 61-62 Jury Directions Act), and unreliability cautions — into the statutory scheme. Only case-specific circumstances (not the mere class of evidence) could ever amount to "substantial and compelling reasons" under s 16 for a further direction.

## What it means for your casework
When you build a sexual offence case in Victoria (and note the same statutory logic applies to the equivalent Evidence Act and jury directions framework), evidence that a complainant was distressed when first complaining is legitimate, admissible support for the prosecution case — both for credibility and as circumstantial evidence that the offence occurred. Gather and document it: witness accounts of the complainant's demeanour, emotional state and words at the time of first complaint are worth capturing in statements. You do not need to worry that the law treats such distress as inherently weak or requiring a special warning; the High Court has expressly rejected that. The jury decides what weight to give it. Be aware the defence may still argue the distress had another cause (as here, the complainant's other difficulties), so record surrounding context so a causal link to the offending remains open on the evidence. The strength of any distress evidence still depends on the usual factors — the complainant's age, timing relative to the alleged offence, and surrounding circumstances.

## Verdict
AWARENESS-level for most detectives, but treat as ACTION where distress at first complaint is part of your evidence: capture and document it fully because it is admissible, credibility-enhancing and probative of the offending.

GAGELER CJ, GORDON, GLEESON, JAGOT AND BEECH-JONES JJ. The
issue of principle raised by this appeal from a decision of the Court of Appeal of
the Supreme Court of Victoria (Beach, Taylor and Orr JJA)1 is whether, in a trial
of a sexual offence governed by the Evidence Act 2008 (Vic) and the Jury
Directions Act 2015 (Vic), where evidence that the complainant was distressed at
the time of making a pre-trial complaint was relied upon by the prosecution to
support the complainant's version of events, there are "substantial and compelling
reasons"2 for the trial judge to warn the jury that: (1) before the evidence of distress
could be used for that purpose the jury had to be satisfied that there was a causal
link between the distress and the alleged offending; and (2) such evidence
generally carries little weight. As will be explained, there are no such reasons and
a trial judge is not required to give such directions.

2

The Court of Appeal was wrong to hold that such directions were required
to be given by the trial judge to the jury in the trial of the respondent in the County
Court of Victoria in which he was found guilty of two offences of incest contrary
to s 44(2) of the Crimes Act 1958 (Vic) and, accordingly, was wrong to hold that
the absence of such directions occasioned a substantial miscarriage of justice
warranting the setting aside of his convictions for those offences under s 276 of
the Criminal Procedure Act 2009 (Vic).

3

In a trial of a sexual offence in Victoria, evidence that a complainant was
distressed at the time of making a pre-trial complaint is ordinarily relevant and
admissible. It is relevant within the meaning of s 55 of the Evidence Act on either
or both of two bases: as evidence that, if accepted, could, first, enhance the credit
of the complainant if the jury were to find a causal connection between the distress
and the making of the complaint3 and, second, support the occurrence of the
offending if the jury were to find a causal connection between the distress and the
offending. On either basis, the evidence could rationally indirectly affect the
assessment of the probability of the existence of a fact in issue – namely, whether
the offending occurred – and is therefore relevant within the meaning of s 55.

The decision of the Court of Appeal
19

The Court of Appeal granted the respondent leave to appeal and allowed
the appeal on a single ground, to the effect that the trial judge's directions in
relation to the use the jury could make of the evidence of the distress of the
complainant at the Children's Court when making the complaint to her mother gave
rise to a substantial miscarriage of justice. In consequence, it quashed the
convictions and ordered a retrial.

20

In so doing, the Court of Appeal drew on its decision in Paull v The Queen,9
delivered before the trial, together with its decisions in Seccull v The King10 and
Nimely (a pseudonym) v The King,11 delivered after the trial, to observe that "[o]n
any view, the causal link between the [respondent's] alleged offending and the
distress exhibited by the complainant at the Children's Court was a weak one". The
Court of Appeal formulated and applied to the case before it the following general
proposition:12
"if a distress direction of the kind given by the [trial] judge was to be given
... [i]t required the trial judge to direct the jury specifically about the need
for the jury to be satisfied that there was a rational causal link between the
distress and the alleged offending; and also to warn the jury of the fact that
distress evidence generally carries little weight".

The correct analysis

24

The requisite statutory analysis is uncomplicated.

25

In Papakosmas v The Queen, McHugh J said:13
"Evidence of distress on the part of a complainant is always relevant, within
the meaning of [s 55 of the Evidence Act], to a charge of sexual assault. A
complainant who has been sexually assaulted may, but will not necessarily,
display outward signs of distress after the assault. Evidence of distress tends
to prove that the complainant had been sexually assaulted."

26

Though it is conceivable that circumstances might exist in which evidence
of distress on the part of a complainant when making a complaint about the charged
offence is not relevant, those circumstances must be rare. As was emphasised by
four members of the Court in IMM v The Queen,14 in which evidence of pre-trial
complaints and of the complainant's distress at the time of making them was held
to be properly admitted for the purpose of proving the occurrence of sexual
offences, a question as to relevance arises "at the point when a piece of evidence

Ordinarily, like evidence of the complaint itself, evidence of distress on the
part of a complainant at the time of making a complaint is evidence that is relevant
in the trial within the meaning of s 55 of the Evidence Act on either or both of two
bases: first, as evidence that, if accepted, could support the credit of the
complainant if the jury were to find a causal connection between the distress and
the making of the complaint, and second, as evidence that, if accepted, could
support the occurrence of the offending if the jury were to find a causal connection
between the distress and the offending. On either basis, the evidence could
rationally indirectly affect the assessment of the probability of the existence of a
fact in issue – namely, whether the offending occurred – and is therefore relevant
within the meaning of s 55.

the evidence of pre-trial distress which has been admitted cannot be the subject of
a request for a particular direction under s 12 of the Jury Directions Act by reason
only of it being evidence of the kind or within the class of pre-trial distress. For
the evidence to become the subject of a particular direction under s 16 of the
Jury Directions Act, the trial judge would need to consider there to be substantial
and compelling reasons for making the direction in the circumstances of the
particular trial. The generic circumstance of the evidence being evidence of the
kind or within the class of pre-trial distress on the part of a complainant could not
alone constitute substantial and compelling reasons.

37

The general direction that the trial judge gave to the jury in the present case
as to the drawing of conclusions and the distinction between direct and
circumstantial evidence sufficed to explain the permissible use by the jury of the
evidence by the complainant and her mother of the complainant's distress at the
time of complaining to her mother that the respondent had raped her. Indeed, the
