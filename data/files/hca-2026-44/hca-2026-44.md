# Estate of Croft v MTH [2026] HCA 44

## One line
The High Court clarifies that a criminal conviction is admissible in later civil proceedings to prove the facts underlying the offence, but only shifts an evidential burden (not the legal onus) and carries no fixed level of probative value.

## What happened
MTH was placed in foster care with the late Geoffrey CROFT in 1979 when she was 16. In 2019 CROFT was convicted of two rapes and three indecent assaults against MTH, and of offences against a boy, RS. MTH sued for damages for assault; CROFT died and his Estate was substituted as defendant. At trial MTH was unrepresented. The Estate admitted the fact of the convictions but denied the abuse. The primary judge (Cavanagh J) wrongly assumed s 91(1) of the Evidence Act 1995 (NSW) barred the convictions being used as proof of the abuse, found MTH not credible, and dismissed her claim. The NSW Court of Appeal allowed her appeal, proceeded 'as if' a certificate of conviction under s 178 had been tendered (none ever was), accepted MTH's account and awarded $1,346,000. The Estate appealed to the High Court.

## What the Court held
The five-judge bench held the primary judge erred by treating s 91(1) as excluding the convictions: the exception in s 92(2) was engaged, so the convictions were admissible as proof of the facts in issue in the criminal proceeding (the elements of each offence). The agreed facts of the convictions were enough — a s 178 certificate is only one way to prove a conviction, not the exclusive or essential means. However, the Court of Appeal wrongly decided the case 'as if' a certificate had been tendered when none existed, and wrongly treated convictions as necessarily having 'high probative value'. A conviction imposes only an evidential burden on the convicted side to point to or produce evidence capable of rebutting the facts — it does not reverse the legal onus, which stays on the plaintiff. Weight varies case by case and must account for the higher criminal standard of proof. The Court also rejected as 'far too absolute' the proposition that a judge must ensure an unrepresented litigant claims all available rights and arguments; the duty is to afford a fair hearing to both sides, not to give running advice. A new trial was ordered.

## What it means for your casework
This is a civil-evidence decision on NSW legislation, so it does not directly govern your WA investigations — WA uses the Evidence Act 1906 (WA), not the uniform Evidence Act 1995. Treat it as background. The useful takeaway for your awareness: in uniform-evidence jurisdictions a conviction can later be used in civil proceedings to prove the underlying facts, and a certificate of conviction is a convenient but not the only proof of a conviction. Understand the distinction the Court drew between an evidential burden (producing enough to raise an issue) and the legal onus (who must ultimately prove the fact) — that distinction matters whenever you think about how a conviction carries into other proceedings. Do not assume a conviction is automatically treated as near-conclusive; its weight depends on what underpinned it.

## Verdict
AWARENESS — it concerns the civil use of convictions under NSW evidence law, which does not change how you investigate or charge in WA, but the convictions-as-evidence and evidential-burden principles are worth knowing.

## Flags (verify before relying)
- This decision interprets the <i>Evidence Act 1995</i> (NSW), ss 91, 92, 178 — WA is NOT a uniform evidence jurisdiction and applies the <i>Evidence Act 1906</i> (WA); the specific sections do not apply in WA.
- Civil (damages) proceeding, not a criminal prosecution — relevance to detective casework is indirect.
- The underlying criminal convictions were made under repealed provisions (<i>Crimes Act 1900</i> (NSW), ss 63 and 76, as at 1979).

Finding of error by the primary judge: Disregard of the evidence of the convictions
62

It follows that the Estate's first ground of appeal to this Court should be
upheld. The Estate's third ground of appeal contends that the Court of Appeal erred
in holding that the primary judge had disregarded the probative weight of a
conviction to prove the elements of an offence when a certificate pursuant to s 178
of the Evidence Act had not been tendered.

63

Leaving aside the Court of Appeal's erroneous focus on the necessity for a
certificate of conviction to be relied on as evidence of convictions, at the core of
its reasoning in determining error on the part of the primary judge that warranted
appellate court intervention were the two related findings described above as the
first step in the Court of Appeal's reasoning. 124 The first finding was that the
primary judge erred in "inferr[ing] that MTH was not seeking to rely on the
convictions against Mr Croft to prove that he had committed the offences for
which he was convicted". The second finding was that the primary judge was
"under a misapprehension ... that a conviction could not prove that the offence had
been committed by the offender". 125 Both of those findings were correct and were
interrelated. Given that MTH included material in her statement referring to the
convictions there was no basis to conclude that she was not relying on the
convictions.

64

Further, throughout the hearing and his judgment it is evident that the
primary judge proceeded on an incorrect understanding that s 91 precluded any
reliance on Mr Croft's convictions as evidence of any facts in issue in his
prosecution. The Estate contended that the admissions made at the hearing as to
the fact of the convictions were not evidence of the facts alleged by MTH in her
statement of claim. However, as explained, 126 the agreed facts concerning the
convictions satisfied s 92(2), and were prima facie evidence of some acts of abuse
of MTH. They were supportive of MTH's case and corroborative of her evidence.

Given that there was error on the part of the primary judge that warranted

appeal by accepting her evidence with respect to the abuse she contended that

70

Consistent with its view that a certificate evidencing convictions was
necessarily evidence of high probative value, the Court of Appeal erred in not
addressing what weight should be attached to that evidence. As noted by the
Interim Report, the "weight to be accorded to a conviction can vary greatly and
should be left to the civil court in each case", 131 although the civil court's
assessment of that weight should account for the high standard of proof applicable
at a criminal trial. 132 Against that, in this case, given that there was not said to be
anyone else present when the sexual and physical abuse was alleged to have taken
place and there was no other direct evidence of the offences, the convictions of
Mr Croft for offences against MTH appear to have been based primarily on an
acceptance of the credibility of her evidence. It is not known whether MTH's
evidence at Mr Croft's trial was supported by other evidence, although the
prosecution case might have been supported by tendency evidence given by RS.
These matters would bear on an assessment of the weight to be attached to the
evidence of Mr Croft's convictions. Of course, other circumstances of Mr Croft's
trial, to the extent they are known, may also bear on that assessment.

71

Relatedly, the Court of Appeal appears to have misunderstood the nature of
the "evidential burden" or onus placed on the Estate. Thus, as noted, the Court
proceeded on the basis that the primary judge was required to start with the
convictions as prima facie evidence of the elements of each offence, to regard
MTH's evidence as corroborated by the (hypothetical) certificate and (critically),
"[t]here being no direct evidence to the contrary" of MTH having been abused, to
treat the convictions as sufficient to discharge MTH's "onus" (that is, her legal
onus) to prove that the abuse perpetrated by Mr Croft occurred. The Court
observed that "it is difficult to conceive of an instance where the [E]state could
displace the evidence of the convictions in circumstances where there were no

The Court of Appeal's approach and that observation not only reflect the
erroneous conclusion of the Court of Appeal that all evidence of convictions is
necessarily of high probative value with the attendant difficulty of displacing that
evidence, but also overstate the effect of the evidential burden imposed on the
Estate. 133 As noted, 134 the substance of that burden only comprised a "burden" of
introducing evidence 135 or drawing attention to the "presence of circumstances"136
that were capable of disproving or rebutting the facts sought to be proved by the
convictions.

73

At the hearing before the primary judge, the Estate's response to MTH's
case at a factual level was to rely on evidence of Mr Croft's denials of wrongdoing,
inconsistencies in MTH's evidence, and discrepancies between her evidence and
that of other witnesses, although the last did not involve or amount to evidence
directly contradicting MTH's evidence as to the occurrence of the rapes and
assaults. The Court of Appeal did not address whether in so doing the Estate
discharged the evidential burden as explained above. In that respect the Court of
Appeal also erred. Moreover neither the primary judge nor the Court of Appeal
addressed the weight to be attached to the evidence of Mr Croft's convictions or
considered whether, in light of that assessment and the balance of the evidence,
MTH discharged her legal onus or burden of proving she was abused by Mr Croft.

74

It follows that the Estate has made out its challenge to the Court of Appeal's
findings resolving MTH's appeal by accepting her evidence on all topics, including
as to damages. The fourth ground of appeal should also be upheld.
