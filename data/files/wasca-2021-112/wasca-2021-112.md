# ISN v The State of Western Australia [2021] WASCA 112

_Held for review — the fact-check found problems with this write-up (pipeline/add_case.py --audit); treat every statement below as unverified until the hold is cleared._

## One line
Recognition evidence from a child complainant who knew the accused well, combined with a strong circumstantial case on identity, upheld a conviction for child sex offences despite a pitch-dark room and no facial recognition.

## What happened
ISN was tried in the District Court before BIRMINGHAM DCJ on an indictment alleging four sexual offences against a girl under 13, called T. Counts 1 and 3 (indecent dealing by kissing) were contrary to Criminal Code (WA) s 320(4); counts 2 and 4 (digital penetration of the vagina) were contrary to s 320(2). The offending occurred at a country town between late 2011 and mid-2013, when T was about 8-10 years old. The accused was a close friend of T's father A and an uncle figure to T, visiting the family home very frequently and often sleeping over, along with his young daughter C, who slept in the same room as T. He was a self-described alcoholic who often drank to excess. T did not complain until 2018. At trial the only real issue was identity: the defence did not dispute that the acts occurred, but argued the accused was not the offender and that other men also visited and drank at the house. The accused denied the offences in a video record of interview (VROI, 25/10/2018) and did not testify; his daughter C gave defence evidence. On 31/10/2019 the jury convicted on all four counts. He was sentenced to a total effective 4 years 6 months' immediate imprisonment. He appealed conviction on four grounds relating to identity directions and the reasonableness of the verdicts.

## What the Court held
The Court of Appeal (BUSS P, MAZZA JA, VAUGHAN JA) refused leave on grounds 1-3, granted leave on ground 4, and dismissed the appeal. On ground 1, T's testimony was properly characterised as recognition evidence (not merely inferential) given her lifelong familiarity with the accused, her very close face-to-face physical contact with the offender, and her evidence of seeing him enter the room and lie next to C; it stood alongside the circumstantial case. On ground 2, a Domican warning is mandated only for identification cases, not ordinarily for recognition cases, because recognition evidence is generally more reliable; the judge properly reminded the jury that honest mistakes in recognition can occur even with close relatives and friends. Although it would have been preferable to specifically isolate the weaknesses in T's recognition evidence, no miscarriage arose given the short trial, the recent closing addresses, and the full Longman direction. On ground 3, the summing up was balanced. On ground 4, the Court held the circumstantial case on identity was the stronger and most compelling category: the only reasonable inference was that the accused was the offender. Evidence based on pure assumption was technically inadmissible but unobjected to and used for the defence's advantage; recognition partly by intuition was admissible (per AK v Western Australia) though of limited weight alone.

## What it means for your casework
This case shows how identity can be proved without any facial recognition where the complainant knew the offender well and a strong circumstantial case exists. When you build a child sex case turning on identity, focus on assembling the combination of circumstances — opportunity, frequency of access, trusted-family-friend status, prior non-sexual touching, and conduct consistent with the offender's behaviour (here, the accused being found in the child's bed, and being the by-far most frequent overnight visitor). The Court found the circumstantial case more compelling than the complainant's recognition evidence itself. Note the distinction between recognition evidence (offender previously known to the witness) and identification evidence (first seen at the crime scene): the full Domican warning is mandated for identification but not ordinarily for recognition. Be aware some of T's evidence about how she 'knew' it was the accused was assumption-based and technically inadmissible — the defence chose not to object because it helped them. To strengthen future cases, gather evidence that rules out alternative male visitors (attendance patterns, who slept over, who had any contact with the child) so the innocence hypothesis is closed off. Delay to complaint (here from ~2011-13 to 2018) triggered a full Longman direction.

## Verdict
AWARENESS: This is an appellate application of settled recognition/identification and unreasonable-verdict principles; it reinforces how to build a circumstantial identity case but does not change investigative procedure.

Ground 2 - legal principles
94

There is a difference between recognition and identification
evidence. The difference was described by Buss JA in Mills v The
State of Western Australia:170
In general, identification evidence describes the evidence of a witness
who identifies an accused as the offender in circumstances where the
witness first saw the accused at or near the crime scene. In general,
recognition evidence describes the evidence of a witness who
recognises an accused as the offender in circumstances where the
accused was previously known to the witness or had previously been
seen by the witness other than at or near the crime scene. Where the
witness's previous knowledge of the accused was tenuous, or the
witness's previous sighting of the accused was fleeting, the witness's
evidence that he or she recognised the accused at or near the crime
scene may, in substance, resemble 'identification evidence'. The nature
and character of the witness's previous connection with the accused is
the crucial issue, rather than the characterisation of his or her evidence
as 'recognition' evidence. (citations omitted)

Ground 4
113

The appellant submitted that T's recognition evidence was 'very

Ground 4 - legal principles
114

The principles governing a ground of appeal that the verdict is
unreasonable or cannot be supported by the evidence, were described
by this court in Wells v The State of Western Australia.184 For
convenience, we will repeat what was written in that case:
The principles governing a ground of appeal that the verdict is
unreasonable or cannot be supported by the evidence are well known.
They may be summarised as follows.

183
184

(1)

the appeal court must undertake its own independent assessment
of the sufficiency and quality of the evidence. It is not simply a
matter of deciding whether as a matter of law there was
evidence to support the verdict. The appeal court must
determine whether, in all the circumstances, it would be
dangerous to permit the verdict to stand;

(2)

the question for the appeal court is whether, upon the whole of
the evidence, it was open to the jury to be satisfied beyond
reasonable doubt that the accused was guilty;

(3)

that question requires consideration of whether the jury must, as
distinct from might, have entertained a reasonable doubt about
the accused's guilt;

(4)

in answering that question, the appeal court must pay full regard
to the consideration that the jury was entrusted with the primary

advantage that the jury had of seeing and hearing the witnesses;
(5)

a doubt experienced by an appellate court would be a doubt
which a jury ought also to have experienced, unless the jury's

The principles relating to criminal cases that turn upon circumstantial
evidence were recently restated by French CJ, Kiefel, Bell, Keane and
Gordon JJ in R v Baden-Clay as follows:
(1)

When the case against an accused rests substantially upon
circumstantial evidence, the jury cannot return a verdict of
guilty unless the circumstances are such to be inconsistent with
any reasonable hypothesis other than the guilt of the accused.

(2)

The jury can be satisfied of the guilt of the accused only if guilt
is not simply a rational inference, but the only rational inference
that the circumstances permit.

(3)

For an inference to be reasonable, it must rest upon something
more than mere conjecture. The bare possibility of innocence
should not prevent a jury from finding an accused person guilty
if the inference of guilt is the only inference open to reasonable
people on a consideration of all the facts in evidence.

(4)

In considering a circumstantial case, all of the circumstances are
to be considered and weighed in deciding whether there is an
inference consistent with innocence reasonably open on the
evidence. The evidence is not to be looked at in a piecemeal
fashion. (citations omitted)

The third component appears to be based on intuition. Insofar as

them, in part, by intuition. In our opinion, having regard to T's
familiarity with the appellant, insofar as her recognition of him
involved a process of intuition, this evidence was admissible, although
not a matter of significant weight by itself.

doubt as to the appellant's guilt on any of the offences. In our opinion,
it was well open to the jury to be satisfied beyond reasonable doubt that
the appellant committed each offence. The present is not a case where
the jury must have entertained a reasonable doubt about the appellant's
guilt. There is no significant possibility that an innocent person has
been convicted. Insofar as the State's case was a circumstantial one, the
only inference reasonably open to the jury was that the appellant was
the perpetrator, and there was no inference consistent with innocence
reasonably open on the evidence. In particular, it was not reasonably
open to conclude that another adult male was the offender.
