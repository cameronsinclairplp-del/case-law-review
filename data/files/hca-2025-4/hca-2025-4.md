# KMD v CEO (Department of Health NT) [2025] HCA 4

## One line
High Court confirms a person under a mental-impairment supervision order has no legal duty to cooperate with medical experts, and refusal cannot make a review 'miscarry'.

## What happened
KMD had been in custody since 07/05/2013, found not guilty of various offences by reason of mental impairment after a special hearing, and was declared liable to supervision under Pt IIA, Div 5 of Sch I to the Criminal Code Act 1983 (NT). A custodial supervision order (CSO) was made in 2015. On a third periodic review, the primary judge (BROWNHILL J) was not satisfied the safety of KMD or the public would be seriously at risk if she were released, and on 05/07/2023 varied the CSO to a non-custodial supervision order (NCSO); KMD was released on 12/07/2023. The CEO (Health) appealed to the NT Court of Criminal Appeal, which (Blokland J dissenting) set aside the NCSO and confirmed the CSO, returning KMD to custody after roughly 12 months in the community. KMD, appearing in person, appealed to the High Court by special leave.

## What the Court held
The Court allowed the appeal. It held the NTCCA majority erred in concluding the review had 'miscarried' because KMD refused to engage with the medical experts. KMD was under no statutory obligation to cooperate with the experts whose reports were provided under ss 43ZK and 43ZN(2)(a); cooperation is not a prerequisite to the reports or to the review under s 43ZH. The judge had to decide, on the evidence available (which included the evidence of non-cooperation), whether she was satisfied the safety of KMD or the public would be seriously at risk — and non-cooperation did not preclude that evaluation. The Court also held that where the NTCCA substitutes a supervision order under s 43ZB(3), it must comply with ss 43ZH(2), 43ZM and 43ZN, and act on the most recent and accurate information reasonably available — here, the 12 months KMD had spent in the community, which the NTCCA ignored. The correctness standard is the correct standard of appellate review for the s 43ZH(2) satisfaction question. Jagot J added that a court cannot keep a person in custody unless it can form the positive satisfaction that safety will be seriously at risk. The matter was remitted to a differently constituted NTCCA.

## What it means for your casework
This is an NT mental-impairment supervision regime, so it will rarely bear directly on WA casework. The practical point worth carrying is that a supervised person's refusal to cooperate with court-appointed medical or psychiatric assessors is not, by itself, a reason to keep them detained — the decision-maker must still evaluate the evidence actually available. If you deal with mentally impaired accused or contribute material to supervision/review proceedings, ensure the evidence you provide is current: courts must decide on the most recent, accurate information, not stale assessments. Do not treat non-cooperation as filling an evidentiary gap.

The statutory scheme

35

The NT Criminal Code contains in Pt IIA a detailed scheme for dealing with
persons who have (or would have) committed an offence but suffer from a mental
impairment meaning that they cannot stand trial or be convicted of the offence.
The scheme requires the Supreme Court of the Northern Territory to make either
a custodial or a non-custodial supervision order in respect of such persons in
certain circumstances.49 The scheme includes provisions for the periodic review of
supervision orders. By s 43ZH(1), after considering a report submitted by an
appropriate person under s 43ZK, "if the court considers it is appropriate, the court
may conduct a review to determine whether the supervised person the subject of
the report may be released from the supervision order". If the review is of a
custodial supervision order then by s 43ZH(2) the Court must either: (a) "vary the
[custodial] supervision order to a non-custodial supervision order unless satisfied
on the evidence available that the safety of the supervised person or the public will
be seriously at risk if the person is released on a non-custodial supervision order";
or (b) "if the court is satisfied on the evidence available that the safety of the
supervised person or the public will be seriously at risk if the person is released on
a non-custodial supervision order" confirm the order or vary the conditions of the
order.

The majority's error
41

The majority's reasoning was impermissible. First, the provisions of the
statutory scheme must be understood to operate together. Accordingly, if on an
appeal the Court of Criminal Appeal decides to "make another supervision order"
in substitution for a supervision order it wishes to quash (which it did in this case),
the Court is making an order under Pt IIA of the NT Criminal Code and must
comply with the provisions of that Part, including ss 43ZH(2), 43ZM and 43ZN.
The majority, however, wrongly treated the Court of Criminal Appeal's powers on
appeal under s 43ZB(3) as standing free from the balance of the provisions of
Pt IIA.

42

Second, had the majority recognised that the Court of Criminal Appeal had
to comply with ss 43ZH(2), 43ZM and 43ZN in quashing the non-custodial
supervision order and making the custodial supervision order (by confirming the
"previous" custodial supervision order Hiley J had made), they also would have
recognised that it was not open to them to reason that because KMD had not
engaged with the medical practitioners who were providing reports to the primary
judge "the position regarding KMD's mental condition and risk assessment had not
fundamentally changed since the reviews conducted by Hiley J".51 KMD had been
released into the community some 12 months before the Court of Criminal Appeal
had delivered judgment, pursuant to the non-custodial supervision order. In those
circumstances the majority could not set aside the non-custodial supervision order
and confirm the previous custodial supervision order because they could not
comply with s 43ZH(2)(a) or (b), s 43ZM or s 43ZN in so doing. In the statutory
scheme providing for the "periodic review" of supervision orders, in s 43ZH(2)(a)
and (b) referring to "on the evidence available", in s 43ZM requiring the Court to
apply the principle set out therein, and in s 43ZN requiring the Court to have regard
to certain matters in determining whether to make such an order, it is to be
understood that the Court is to ensure that it acts on the basis of "the most recent
and accurate information"52 reasonably available to the Court.

Fifth, and finally, the terms of s 43ZH(2)(a) and (b) expose why it cannot

minimum that is consistent with maintaining and protecting the safety of the
community". Consistently with this, on the Court completing a review of a
custodial supervision order, the Court must vary the custodial supervision order to
a non-custodial supervision order unless satisfied on the evidence available that
the safety of the supervised person or the public will be seriously at risk if the
person is released on a non-custodial supervision order (s 43ZH(2)(a)). It is only
if the Court is satisfied on the evidence available that the safety of the supervised
person or the public will be seriously at risk if the person is released on a noncustodial supervision order that the Court must confirm or vary the conditions only

must vary the custodial supervision order to a non-custodial supervision order. The
Court cannot order that a person be kept in custody under a custodial supervision
