# WHS v The King [2025] HCA 51

## One line
The prosecution merely leading a child complainant's age and not adducing evidence of prior sexual experience does not "disclose or imply" a lack of sexual experience so as to unlock cross-examination under s 293(6) of the <i>Criminal Procedure Act 1986</i> (NSW).

## What happened
WHS was convicted at a 2023 retrial (the second trial) of four sexual offences against MW, a child under ten. The offending was alleged between 27/01/2010 and 10/11/2012; MW first described the abuse to police on 13-14/11/2012 when aged nine. Before trial, WHS applied to adduce FACS records and a 2017 police interview suggesting MW had displayed sexualised behaviour and been exposed to sexual matters by others (including a young male, AT), and to cross-examine MW about viewing pornographic movies with cousins. This material was said to explain how a nine-year-old could describe the abuse in detail. The trial judge ruled it inadmissible under s 293(3). The Court of Criminal Appeal quashed two convictions (Counts 1 and 4) as unreasonable but upheld Counts 2 and 5, and rejected the s 293 argument and a complaint about the Crown's closing address. WHS appealed to the High Court.

## What the Court held
The appeal was dismissed. The "case for the prosecution" in s 293(6) is not confined to the prosecutor's opening or closing address — it embraces the entirety of how the case is presented, including implicit steps in the reasoning towards guilt. However, there is a critical distinction: a prosecution case that discloses or implies a lack of sexual experience is different from the prosecution merely adducing evidence of a complainant whose age alone creates a risk the jury will assume or infer a lack of sexual experience. The latter does not engage s 293(6). The prosecution has no free-standing power to adduce sexual-experience evidence; s 293(3) binds it, so the required "disclosure or implication" ordinarily arises only where the prosecution has obtained a ruling and led such evidence under a s 293(4) exception. A mere risk that the jury might assume or infer a lack of prior sexual experience from the complainant's age and the absence of such evidence is not sufficient. Accepting WHS's argument would engage s 293(6) in effectively every case involving a complainant aged nine or under, gutting the statutory protection for a vulnerable class. On Ground 2, the four excluded FACS documents did not contradict or weaken the Crown's closing submission explaining MW's delay in complaining, so no unfairness or miscarriage was established.

## What it means for your casework
When you are building a child sexual assault brief in NSW, understand that s 293 (now s 294CB) strongly restricts any evidence of a complainant's prior sexual experience or activity, and this protection is at its strongest for young children. The prosecution cannot freely lead such evidence to bolster the case — it must fit a s 293(4) exception and obtain a court ruling. For your investigative approach, do not assume that material suggesting a child had prior sexual exposure (which might "explain" their knowledge) will ever reach the jury: the defence cannot cross-examine on it just because the child's young age might lead jurors to infer sexual naivety. The pathway to cross-examination only opens where the prosecution actually discloses or implies a lack of experience through evidence led or submissions made — not from age alone or from what evidence was left out. Practically, be careful in your dealings and disclosure obligations: this case grew from earlier convictions being quashed because potentially exculpatory FACS material was not disclosed in answer to a subpoena. Ensure all relevant records are identified and disclosed even where admissibility is contested. Also note a prosecutor's closing that explains delayed complaint must fairly reflect the evidence.

## Verdict
AWARENESS-level authority: it clarifies the law on admissibility and cross-examination limits rather than changing your day-to-day evidence-gathering, but the disclosure lesson underneath it is directly relevant to how you build a brief.
